One C3 Solve, Start to Finish

  1. Build the problem. Relinearize around the current real state → get the LCS matrices (A, B, D, E, F, H, c). Happens every relin_period_steps control ticks, not every solve.
  2. Split it in two. The true problem is non-convex — a force-times-gap-equals-zero constraint can't go in a normal QP — so make two copies of the trajectory: z (must obey dynamics/bounds) and δ (must obey complementarity). Add a memory variable w to track how far apart they drift.
  3. z-update. With δ and w held fixed, solve one real convex QP for z: the true MPC cost, plus a penalty (weight G) for disagreeing with δ. One call to OSQP — the expensive step. (§1, §4)
  4. δ-update. With the new z held fixed, snap onto the complementarity-legal set. For C3+ this is instant closed-form arithmetic, no solver at all. (§0, §3)
  5. w-update. w is a running total of every past round's leftover gap: w ← w + (z−δ). Next round's z-update then aims at δ−w instead of plain δ — so a consistent bias gets cancelled permanently instead of rediscovered from scratch each round, which is what lets z and δ eventually land exactly together, not just close. Then next round's "agree with each other" penalty is made stiffer too: G *= rho_scale, and w is rescaled to match. (§1)
  6. Repeat steps 3-5, admm_iter times. Because G grows exponentially, only the first ≈ log(1000)/log(rho_scale) rounds actually move the answer — after that the penalty dominates and the solve is effectively frozen. (§1, §2)
  7. One last z-update after the loop ends, so the solve finishes on a dynamics-consistent trajectory rather than a δ.
  8. Only the first knot ships. The plan covers N knots, but only u_0/x_0 is sent to the robot. Everything past that is discarded and replanned from scratch next solve, every c3_period_steps control ticks.
§0

Three Nested Solves

Almost every confusing interaction between parameters comes from not knowing which of three nested loops a given flag lives in. The three are nested like Russian dolls: one call to C3::Solve() runs the outer loop; every single round of the outer loop makes one call into the middle loop; that one call itself runs the inner loop start to finish before returning. Concretely, for one solve at the defaults below (admm_iter=20, max_iter=4000): the outer loop runs 20 times, each of those 20 times triggers one full solve of the middle loop, and each of those solves can itself take up to 4000 internal steps. Three completely different, unrelated things are all called an "iteration" here — that's the source of almost every confusing question about these flags. From the outside in:

Loop 1 — Outer · runs once per C3 solve
C3's own ADMM
admm_iter rounds of z-update → δ-update → w-update. This is the layer that takes the hard, non-convex problem and splits it into two easy pieces it alternates between — described from scratch in §1.
↓ each round of Loop 1 calls this once
Loop 2 — the z-update · runs once per Loop-1 round
One call to OSQP
A real, standard convex QP (no complementarity, just dynamics and bounds), handed to the OSQP solver and solved to osqp_eps tolerance. This is the expensive step — the one actually doing the numerical heavy lifting.
↓ that one call runs this internally
Loop 3 — inside OSQP · runs once per Loop-2 call
OSQP's own internal iteration
OSQP is, internally, its own ADMM-family algorithm — the same splitting trick one level up, used to solve one plain QP fast. It has its own internal rho/sigma/alpha and can take up to max_iter steps before returning its answer back up to Loop 1.

One thing doesn't fit neatly on this ladder: the δ-update (the projection onto the complementarity set) also happens once per Loop-1 round, right alongside the z-update above — but for C3+ (the default here) it isn't a solve at all, it's a picture worth actually drawing.

The picture: an L-shaped legal zone on a 2D plane

Put λ (the force) on the x-axis and η (the gap) on the y-axis. The rule \(0\leq\lambda\perp\eta\geq 0\) means exactly one of the two can be positive, never both, never negative. On these axes that legal set is exactly two rays glued together at the origin — the positive λ-axis, and the positive η-axis. A right angle, an upside-down L. That's the entire legal region; everywhere else on the plane — both positive, either negative — is forbidden.

Loop 2's z-update hands back some point \((\lambda^\circ,\eta^\circ)=z+w\) that has no idea this L exists — it solved a plain QP, nothing about complementarity. That point can land anywhere on the plane, usually not on the L. The δ-update's whole job: snap it to the nearest legal point on the L.

The actual rule, matching SolveSingleProjection (c3_plus.cc:204-218) exactly, is two steps, not one:

1. Compare \(\lambda^\circ\) and \(\eta^\circ\). Whichever is bigger, keep it. Set the other to exactly 0.
2. Clip whatever's left: if it's still negative, squash it to 0 too.

Three landing zones, worked with numbers:

  • (λ°,η°) = (5, 3) — both positive, λ bigger. Step 1 keeps λ=5, zeros η → (5, 0). Step 2 changes nothing (5 is already ≥0). Lands on the λ-axis.
  • (λ°,η°) = (3, 5) — η bigger. Step 1 keeps η=5, zeros λ → (0, 5). Lands on the η-axis.
  • (λ°,η°) = (−5, −3) — both negative. −3 is "bigger" (less negative), so step 1 keeps η=−3, zeros λ → (0, −3). Step 2 then clips that surviving −3 down to 0 → (0, 0), the corner where the two rays meet.

That combination is the exact geometric nearest-point snap onto the L — not an approximation, just computed by one comparison instead of by solving a distance-minimization problem. It's plain arithmetic: no solver, no tolerance, no iteration count. Keep that in mind for §3 — it means some parameters that sound like they should shape the projection's answer only shape which side it lands on, not how far out.

The name collision to watch for

Loop 1 has a parameter called rho_scale. Loop 3 (OSQP, set up at allegro_grasp_c3_squeeze.cc:1340) has its own, completely unrelated parameter also called rho. They are not the same number, do not affect the same thing, and changing one does nothing to the other. Every time this doc says "rho_scale," it means Loop 1's; every time it says "OSQP's rho," it means Loop 3's. See §4.

§1

G, w, rho_scale — From Scratch

This is the single most consequential parameter group in the whole system, and the root cause of the worst bug this session hit. Before any code: what problem are G, w, and rho_scale even for? Built up from nothing, with every symbol defined before it's used.

The problem: two answers that need to agree

ADMM (companion doc §5) keeps two copies of the trajectory. \(z\) is solved to satisfy the easy constraints — dynamics, box bounds — call this the z-update. \(\delta\) is solved to satisfy the hard constraint — complementarity — the δ-update. Each is solved separately because each, by itself, is easy. The catch: with nothing tying them together, solving \(z\) with zero regard for \(\delta\) just gives the best trajectory while completely ignoring complementarity — exactly the problem we were trying to avoid in the first place.

Fix 1: penalize disagreement — this is what G is

Add a term to the z-update's cost that punishes \(z\) for being far from \(\delta\):

\[ \min_{z}\;\; c(z) \;+\; \frac{G}{2}\,\|z - \delta\|^2 \]

Read left to right: \(c(z)\) is the real thing you care about — the MPC cost from §6, does the plan track the goal, avoid huge forces. \(\|z-\delta\|^2\) is one number measuring how far apart the two copies currently sit — zero if they match, growing the further apart they get. \(G\) is just a number multiplying that distance. That's the entire idea of \(G\) — a dial with two extremes:

G = 0

The penalty vanishes. This is "solve for \(z\) with no regard for \(\delta\)" — back to the broken starting point.

G = one million

Any disagreement \(\|z-\delta\|^2>0\), even tiny, gets multiplied by a million and becomes enormous. The optimizer sacrifices almost anything in \(c(z)\) to avoid it — \(z\) ends up equal to \(\delta\), full stop.

So \(G\) is literally how much do we care about agreement, relative to how much we care about the plan being good. The flag w_G sets \(G\)'s starting value — the flag and the math symbol are related but not the same thing: w_G is a number you pass on the command line; \(G\) is what it becomes inside the solver, and (as §1 continues below) it does not stay at that starting value.

What "the coefficient dwarfs the cost" means — the actual mechanism

This isn't a figure of speech — it falls straight out of calculus, and it's worth deriving once so it's not a black box. To minimize a smooth function, set its derivative to zero. Do that to the boxed expression above, treating \(\delta\) as a fixed constant (it's held fixed during the z-update):

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Bigl[c(z) + \tfrac{G}{2}\|z-\delta\|^2\Bigr] = \nabla c(z) + G(z-\delta) = 0 \]

Solve that one line for \(z\):

\[ z \;=\; \delta \;-\; \frac{\nabla c(z)}{G} \]

Look at what this says: the optimal \(z\) equals \(\delta\), corrected by a term that shrinks as \(G\) grows. \(\nabla c(z)\) — the gradient, the direction the true cost wants to move — is fixed by the actual physics and goals of the problem. But it's divided by \(G\). Make \(G\) large enough and \(\nabla c(z)/G\) rounds down to nothing. The true cost's entire influence on the answer gets divided away, leaving \(z \approx \delta\) regardless of what the real objective wanted. That is exactly what "the coefficient dwarfs the cost" means: a literal division in the optimality condition, zeroing out the one place the real objective enters the answer.

A number to make this concrete

This session's own logs put a typical per-knot cost \(c(z)\) in the tens to low hundreds (any COST PER KNOT table). Say \(\|\nabla c(z)\| \sim 100\) and \(z,\delta\) differ by 1cm (0.01) in some coordinate. At \(G=1\), the correction \(\nabla c(z)/G \approx 100\) — bigger than the gap itself, so the true cost has real say in where \(z\) lands. At \(G=1000\), the same correction is \(100/1000=0.1\) — already smaller than the 1cm gap it's supposed to help close. The true cost has been divided down to a rounding error.

Fix 2: a finite G alone leaves a permanent gap — this is what w fixes

Problem with fix 1 as the whole story: at any finite \(G\), the equation \(z = \delta - \nabla c(z)/G\) says \(z\) always sits slightly off from \(\delta\) — by exactly \(\nabla c(z)/G\), which never actually reaches zero. Alternately solving \(z\) pulled toward \(\delta\), then \(\delta\) pulled toward \(z\), with only this plain penalty, can circle forever, always a hair apart, never landing on one exact shared answer.

The fix is a second variable \(w\) — a running memory of the gap. Penalize \(\|z - \delta + w\|^2\) instead of \(\|z-\delta\|^2\), and after every round update \(w\) by adding whatever gap was just observed:

\[ w \;\leftarrow\; w + (z - \delta) \]

Read this as a ledger. If \(z\) keeps landing slightly on one side of \(\delta\), round after round, \(w\) keeps accumulating that same offset, growing a little each iteration. That growing \(w\) then appears inside next round's penalty term, nudging \(z\) a little further to compensate for the persistent bias. Once \(z\) and \(\delta\) finally land on the exact same point, \((z-\delta)=0\), so \(w\) stops changing — it has found the exact extra push needed to make the two independent subproblems agree perfectly, not just approximately. One-sentence version: \(G\) is the stiffness of the spring pulling \(z\) and \(\delta\) together; \(w\) is how far that spring is currently stretched.

The actual code, and why rho_scale touches G and w together

Now C3::ADMMStep (c3_src/core/c3.cc:419-447), verbatim — every single ADMM iteration, right after the z-update and δ-update above run once each: 

for (int i = 0; i < N_; ++i) {
  w->at(i) = w->at(i) + z[i] - delta->at(i);   // the ledger update from Fix 2, above
  w->at(i) = w->at(i) / options_.rho_scale;     // then rescaled down...
  G->at(i) = G->at(i) * options_.rho_scale;      // ...as G is scaled up
}

Two things happen together, and they're linked, not two separate mechanisms. Multiplying G makes the spring stiffer — pulls \(z\) and \(\delta\) together harder next round. But \(w\) (the ledger) was accumulated under the old, softer spring — it represents a stretch calibrated to the old stiffness. Make the spring stiffer without adjusting the stored stretch and the same stored number now represents a very different real force. Dividing \(w\) by the same rho_scale that multiplies \(G\) keeps the actual force being applied (roughly \(G \times w\)) consistent as the stiffness dial turns — recalibrating the ledger's units each time the spring changes, not bolting on a second independent mechanism.

G starts at w_G · I and gets multiplied by rho_scale every single ADMM iteration — not added to, multiplied. After i iterations:

\[ G_i = w_G \cdot \text{rho\_scale}^{\,i} \]

Combine this with the amber card above and the picture is complete: every ADMM round, the "spring" gets exponentially stiffer, which (by the derivation two sections up) exponentially divides away the true cost's influence on the z-update's answer. The flag's own help text (allegro_grasp_c3_squeeze.cc:325-330) states the resulting rule of thumb, and it's exact — exponential growth means the number of iterations before the cost's influence is gone is a logarithm, not a fraction of admm_iter:

\[ \text{useful iterations} \approx \frac{\ln(1000)}{\ln(\text{rho\_scale})} \]
rho_scaleIterations until G ≈ 1000×admm_iter needed to actually use them
1.2~38≥ 40
1.08~90≥ 90
1.04~176≥ 175

What actually happened this session

rho_scale=3 was the original value. \(\ln(1000)/\ln(3) \approx 6.3\) — ADMM was effectively frozen after 7 iterations no matter what admm_iter was set to. Every extra iteration beyond ~7 was solving a QP whose consensus penalty so overwhelmed the true cost that the answer barely moved. This looked exactly like "the plan is just bad" — the real cause was a mistuned exponent, not a bug in the physics, the LCS, or the executor. Fixed by exposing --rho_scale as a flag and dropping it to 1.08-1.2.

The corollary that mattered just as much, later: if you raise the cost weights (Q), you must raise w_G to match, or lower rho_scale/raise admm_iter to compensate — because G's starting point is w_G, and it needs to reach a magnitude comparable to Q before the z-update's proximal term can actually force agreement. Jumping k_hold (a Q-block weight, §6) from 10 to 2000 — a 200× increase — while leaving w_G=1.5 unchanged broke convergence outright: λ at the executed knot started sign-flipping solve to solve (0.81 → 1.23 → −0.17 → 0.01 → −1.16 N across nine consecutive 40ms-apart solves, on a cube that wasn't moving). Raising w_G to 10 fixed it. G and Q are not automatically kept in balance by the solver — that's a manual tuning invariant you have to maintain yourself.

Three knobs can technically raise G_final = w_G · rho_scale^admm_iter to match a bigger Q, but they are not equally safe:

LeverEffectVerdict
w_G ↑ Scales G at every iteration by the same factor — same ramp shape, just shifted up. What actually fixed the 200× jump above, with only a ~7× bump (the exponential ramp itself supplies the rest). Preferred — direct, free, no extra compute
admm_iter ↑ Lets the same ramp run longer, reaching a bigger G just by more rounds. Has a ceiling: past ≈ ln(1000)/ln(rho_scale) rounds (§1's table), G is already ~1000× its start and further rounds barely add anything (§2). Fine, but costs real solve time and has a ceiling
rho_scale ↑ Reaches a given G in fewer rounds — but rho_scale also directly sets how many rounds are useful before the freeze, and a bigger rho_scale means fewer of them. This is literally the mechanism behind the original rho_scale=3 bug. Trap — trades "G is big enough" for "not enough real rounds happened"
§2

admm_iter: How Many Iterations Actually Matter

admm_iter is a hard iteration count, not a convergence tolerance — c3.cc:374 runs exactly this many ADMMStep calls, then does one final z-update and stops (c3.cc:383, is_final_solve = true). There is no early-exit-on-convergence check. Two failure modes, both real this session, sit on either side of the "right" value:

Too few

Before G has grown enough for the z/δ copies to actually agree, the returned trajectory can satisfy dynamics (z) or complementarity (δ) but not both well. Symptom: the diagnostic block's [ADMM consensus ‖z−δ‖] line stays large (not ≈0), and min(λ)/min(η) in [complementarity(δ)] read negative — the returned λ literally doesn't satisfy the LCP it's supposed to.

Too many, relative to rho_scale

Past the \(\ln(1000)/\ln(\text{rho\_scale})\) point (§1), extra iterations cost real wall-clock time inside the ~50ms control budget for essentially zero improvement — G is already so large the QP answer has stopped changing. Not incorrect, just wasted compute that could go to a finer osqp_eps or a shorter c3_period_steps instead.

Practical rule: pick rho_scale first (how many effective iterations you want), then set admm_iter to at least that many — and re-check both together whenever you change Q-side weights (§1's corollary).

§3

w_G vs w_U: Two Weights That Behave Completely Differently

Both are described identically in the flag list — "ADMM augmented-Lagrangian weight" — which makes them look symmetric. They are not. Trace what ADMMStep actually does with each:

w_Gw_U
Initial value w_G · I w_U · I
Ramped by rho_scale? Yes — every iteration (c3.cc:445) No — never touched inside ADMMStep
Used where z-update's proximal penalty (always) δ-update's projection metric — only if nonzero. If U is all-zero, SolveProjection falls back to using (the already-ramped) G instead (c3.cc:436-439).
Effective magnitude after a solve Explodes to \(w_G\cdot\text{rho\_scale}^{\,i}\) — different every iteration Constant, exactly w_U, for the whole solve

What U actually is, in plain terms — before any code

U never appears in the headline z/δ/w story in §0-§1, and that's not an oversight on a first read — it genuinely isn't part of that picture. It's a narrow tool used at exactly one moment, described here using only λ and η, no new symbols.

The z-update spits out some candidate \((\lambda^\circ, \eta^\circ)\) — a force number and a gap number. It might come back as, say, \((50, 0.02)\): both positive, which is illegal (only one of the two is ever allowed to be positive). Someone has to decide, right then: is this really a "there's contact, force wins, the gap number should be zeroed out" situation, or a "there's a gap, no force, the force number should be zeroed out" situation? Picking that winner is the entire job of the δ-update, every round.

The naive way to decide: just compare the two raw numbers — \(50 > 0.02\), so force wins. The problem: λ is measured in Newtons, η in a completely different, length-like unit. Comparing \(50\) to \(0.02\) directly is like comparing 50 dollars to 0.02 euros and declaring dollars bigger — meaningless until an exchange rate is fixed, because the raw numbers are artifacts of which units happened to be used, not a real statement about which one deserves to win.

That's the entire conceptual job of U: it's the exchange rate. A number, one per contact, that says "multiply λ and η by this before comparing them, so the comparison is fair." Nothing more. It has nothing to do with the z-update, nothing to do with G's job of pulling \(z\) toward \(\delta\) (§1) — it exists solely to make one specific comparison, at one specific moment, meaningful.

For C3+ specifically: w_U's number is inert — only zero-vs-nonzero matters

SolveProjection for C3+ dispatches to c3_plus.cc's SolveSingleProjection, verbatim (c3_plus.cc:204-218): 

// Set the smaller of lambda and eta to zero
eta_larger = eta_c*sqrt(w_eta) > lambda_c*sqrt(w_lambda);
lambda_slot = eta_larger ? 0 : lambda_c;
eta_slot    = eta_larger ? eta_c : 0;
// Clip whatever survived at 0
lambda_slot = max(lambda_slot, 0);
eta_slot    = max(eta_slot, 0);

w_eta/w_lambda are not separate flags — they're names for two slices of whichever matrix this function was handed, three naming levels down from the command line:

  1. The flag. w_G/w_U — a single number you set on the command line.
  2. The full matrix. Each flag is expanded into an entire matrix, allegro_grasp_c3_squeeze.cc:1275-1278: G_vec = w_G · Identity(n_z,n_z), U_vec = w_U · Identity(n_z,n_z) — uniform, covering the whole z-vector (x, λ, u, η together), no per-block distinction yet.
  3. The slice. SolveSingleProjection receives one of those two matrices (whichever c3.cc:436 picked) and reads two small pieces out of it: the rows lining up with η, called w_eta; the rows lining up with λ, called w_lambda.

Since step 2 built a uniform matrix — the same number on every diagonal entry — the two slices pulled out in step 3 are always identical to each other, and both equal whichever flag built the matrix. That's why sqrt(w_eta) and sqrt(w_lambda) are the same positive factor on both sides of the comparison, and cancel completely: the comparison reduces to plain, unweighted eta_c > lambda_c no matter what w_U's value is.

One more wrinkle if you go reading c3_plus.cc directly: SolveSingleProjection's first parameter is declared const MatrixXd& U in the function signature, always — regardless of whether the caller actually passed G or U at the call site. The local variable name U inside that function does not tell you which flag's data is in it; only the caller (c3.cc:436-439) does.

So the honest conclusion, verified rather than guessed: for C3+ under this codebase's current construction, w_U's specific number has no effect on the projection's outcome at all. Only whether it's exactly zero or not (the c3.cc:436 gate) does anything — and what that gate actually buys you is a tie-break that stays flat and fixed round to round, decoupled from G's exponential growth (§1), rather than "favor λ over η" or vice versa as the name w_U might suggest. That would only start being true if G_vec/U_vec were built to weight the λ- and η-blocks differently — the code supports it structurally, but this flag doesn't exercise it. Matters far more for C3QP/C3MIQP, where the projection is a real weighted distance minimization and any asymmetry in the metric shapes the answer directly.

§4

OSQP: the Solver Inside the Solver

Every z-update (Loop 2, §0) is one call into OSQP (Operator Splitting Quadratic Program solver), via Drake's drake::solvers::OsqpSolver. OSQP is itself an ADMM-family algorithm — it splits the QP into an equality-constrained linear system and a simple box-projection, and alternates between them, exactly the same shape of trick C3 uses one level up. That's why it's fast and warm-startable: each z-update's QP differs from the previous ADMM iteration's only in the right-hand side (a new δ − w), so OSQP can resume from its last internal state instead of starting cold.

The full option block, set once per relinearization at allegro_grasp_c3_squeeze.cc:1329-1345:

It means individual QPs are being solved loosely, independent of how many ADMM rounds run.

§5

Two Different "Warm Start"s

Two independent flags, easy to conflate:

--warm_start

Within one solve

Seeds each ADMM iteration's z-update QP with the previous iteration's solution as OSQP's initial guess (SetInitialGuessQP, c3.cc:449). Helps OSQP converge faster inside a single Solve() call. Has no memory across separate Solve() calls.

--warm_start_admm

Across separate solves

Carries the ADMM δ/w state from the end of one Solve() into the start of the next (c3.cc:349-361), instead of cold-starting δ=0, w=0 every ~40ms control step. Anchors each new solve near the previous plan — a low-pass on solve-to-solve churn. Directly relevant to solve-to-solve λ jitter (§9): if λ at the executed knot is sign-flipping between consecutive solves on a barely-moving system, a cold ADMM restart landing in a different point of an indeterminate force-nullspace is a real candidate cause, and this flag is the first thing to try.

§6

Cost Weights: Q and R

These decide what the plan wants. They are a completely separate axis from G/U (§1/§3), which decide how hard ADMM enforces agreement between its two internal copies — the same distinction as the companion doc's §0 correction that D, not Q, couples forces to motion. Raising a Q-block weight never by itself helps ADMM converge; it can actively hurt convergence unless G is raised too (§1).

w_cube10000.0Cube pose error only. The load-bearing term — this is what forces Σμλ_n ≥ mg in the first place.
w_cube_vel0.0Cube velocity error, split out from w_cube so it can't dominate and drown out the position anchor (an earlier, since-fixed bug: both were folded into one weight at 1000, and the velocity term won).

mu0.5Friction coefficient in the LCS. Directly scales how much normal force converts to usable friction — the μΣλ_n ≥ mg requirement.
num_friction_directions2Edges in the polyhedral friction-cone approximation per contact. Changes n_λ (and therefore n_z) directly: n_λ = n_c · (1 + 1 + 2·num_friction_directions) for Stewart-Trinkle. More directions = a rounder cone approximation and a bigger QP.
contact_modelstewart_and_trinkleSwitches the entire D/E/F/H/c construction (see companion doc §7 of lcs_factory.cc). anitescu is a convex reformulation where normal force is the sum of cone-edge forces, avoiding the explicit complementarity gate on λ_n's cap that Stewart-Trinkle enforces — a structurally different lever than any solver-tuning flag, held in reserve this session.
N5Horizon length in knots. Every extra knot adds a full block of (x,λ,u,η) to the z-update QP — linear cost in problem size, but also more room for the frozen linearization to go stale by the last knot.
c3_dt0.05LCS timestep. Appears squared in the F-matrix's force→gap coupling (companion doc §7) — halving c3_dt roughly quadruples the achievable λ_n at a given penetration depth. The other lever (besides commanded penetration) for escaping the "λ0≈0 at zero penetration" complementarity cap.
input_scale0.0 (auto)Rescales the optimized input ũ so τ=s_u·ũ and |B_scaled|≈|A| — a QP-conditioning change of variables, not a physical one. λ is untouched by this.
§8

Cadence and Compute Knobs

c3_period_steps (default 40)

Run a full C3 solve once every this many 1kHz control ticks. Does not change the plan itself, only how often it's recomputed. Between solves, the previous solution is reused — the executor is still reading the same xplan[0]/λ0 for the whole window.

relin_period_steps (default 40)

Re-linearize the LCS (fresh φ/Jn/Jt from real geometry) this often. Keep it a multiple of c3_period_steps so every solve gets a fresh LCS rather than solving against an increasingly stale one.

§9

Diagnosing Convergence: a Playbook

Reading --plan_debug's SOLVER DIAG block against this table usually identifies which of the three loops (§0) is actually at fault, before touching a single flag:

SymptomLikely layerFirst thing to try
[ADMM consensus ‖z−δ‖] stays large across the whole solve Loop 1 — not enough effective ADMM iterations Check rho_scale vs admm_iter against the §1 table first. If they're already matched, raise w_G.
min(λ) or min(η) reads clearly negative Loop 1 — solve stopped before z/δ agreed Same as above. A negative λ or η in the reported solution is not a valid LCP point at all.
[dynamics residual] (‖x_{k+1}−f(...)‖) is large Loop 2 — the z-update QP itself wasn't solved tightly Tighten osqp_eps. This is independent of admm_iter/rho_scale — don't chase it there.
λ0 sign-flips or swings by 10-100× between consecutive ~40ms solves on a system that isn't moving Loop 1 — cold-started ADMM landing in different points of an indeterminate solution manifold each time Try --warm_start_admm=true (§5) before anything else — this is exactly the symptom it targets.
Recently raised a Q-block weight (k_hold, w_cube, ...) and convergence got worse Loop 1 — Q/G imbalance (§1's corollary) Raise w_G proportionally. A 200× Q jump needs roughly a 200× (or log-scale, via more admm_iter) G response, not a 2× one.
OSQP itself reports infeasible/unbounded Problem formulation, not tuning Check for missing box bounds on u (an unbounded primal looks exactly like this) before touching any tolerance.

The fastest way to misdiagnose this system is to treat every bad plan as a physics problem or an executor problem before checking whether ADMM actually converged at all. Read ‖z−δ‖ first.
