§0

Setup, Conventions, and the Three Fingers

We synthesise a grasp of a free-floating cube with the Allegro hand by solving a single trajectory optimization over a horizon of \(N = 100\) timesteps. The ring finger is ignored entirely; only three fingers participate:

FingerSymbolTip bodyCube face (from cube_kinematics.h)
Index \(I\)link_3_tip \(-Y\) face
Middle \(M\)link_7_tip \(-Y\) face
Thumb \(T\)link_15_tip \(+Y\) face (opposing)

Index and middle press one face; the thumb opposes them on the other — the two-versus-one antipodal squeeze whose force balance is derived in §5 of the grasp-theory document. Throughout, an index \(i \in \{I, M, T\}\) ranges over the three fingers and \(k \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\) over the horizon.

State

\(\mathbf{x}_k = (\mathbf{q}_k,\ \dot{\mathbf{q}}_k)\)

\(\mathbf{q}_k\) stacks the Allegro joint angles (index, middle, thumb) and the cube's free-body pose (position + quaternion); \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}_k\) the corresponding generalised velocities. This is exactly the LCS state of §2 of the solver document.

§1

Decision Variables

The timestep \(\Delta t\) is a fixed parameter, as in C3 — not a decision variable. The optimizer solves over the full trajectory of the following:

\(\mathbf{q}_{0:N}\)   — configuration trajectory (hand joints + cube pose), inside \(\mathbf{x}_k\)

\(\mathbf{u}_{0:N-1}\)   — joint-torque controls applied to the three fingers

\(\lambda_{n,k}^{i} \equiv f_k^{i} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)   — normal contact-force magnitude of finger \(i\)

\(\boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{n_d}\)   — tangential friction weights, one per edge of the \(n_d\)-sided friction pyramid

\(\gamma_k^{i} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)   — sliding-speed slack (one per contact)

\(\mathbf{x}_k^{i} \in \mathbb{R}^3\)   — fingertip contact points in the world frame, tied to \(\mathbf{q}_k\) by FK (§4)

The contact variable bundle

Following C3, all per-finger contact quantities are stacked into one vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\), and the three fingers concatenate into the full \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) that drives the dynamics:

\[ \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^{i} = \bigl(\,\lambda_{n,k}^{i},\ \boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i},\ \gamma_k^{i}\,\bigr), \qquad \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k = \bigl(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^{I},\ \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^{M},\ \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^{T}\bigr). \]

The scalar \(f_k^{i}\) used in the objective is precisely the normal entry \(\lambda_{n,k}^{i}\). The tangential force is recovered from the weights as \(\mathbf{f}_{t,k}^{i} = D^{i}\boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i}\), where the columns of \(D^{i} = [\,\mathbf{d}_1^{i}\ \cdots\ \mathbf{d}_{n_d}^{i}\,]\) are the friction-pyramid direction vectors spanning the contact tangent plane (§4).

§2

The Objective Function

The cost sums three intentions over the fixed horizon — reaching the target grasp points, exerting the target forces, and keeping the control effort small:

\[ J \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Bigl[\, \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{i} w_p\,\bigl\|\mathbf{x}_k^{i}-\mathbf{x}_\star^{i}\bigr\|^2}_{\text{position tracking}} \;+\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{i} w_f\,\bigl(f_k^{i}-\alpha_i\bigr)^2}_{\text{force tracking}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbf{u}_k^\top R\,\mathbf{u}_k}_{\text{control cost}} \,\Bigr] \]

The targets \(\mathbf{x}_\star^{I},\mathbf{x}_\star^{M},\mathbf{x}_\star^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^3\) are the grasp contact points from GetGraspPositions (cube_kinematics.h), defined relative to the cube so \(\mathbf{x}_\star^{i} = X_{WC}(\mathbf{q}_k)\,\mathbf{r}^{i}\) tracks the cube pose; \(w_p, w_f > 0\) and \(R \succ 0\) trade off the three intentions.

Term 1 — position tracking

Drives each fingertip \(\mathbf{x}_k^{i} = \mathrm{FK}_i(\mathbf{q}_k)\) toward its target grasp point \(\mathbf{x}_\star^{i}\) — the fingers converge to the contact geometry rather than only modulating force once they touch.

Term 2 — force tracking

Drives each normal force \(f_k^{i}\) toward its force-closure target \(\alpha_i\) (§3), so the converged grasp exerts a balanced squeeze, not an arbitrary one.

Term 3 — control cost

The quadratic \(\mathbf{u}_k^\top R\,\mathbf{u}_k\) penalises joint-torque effort — the standard C3 \(R\)-cost. It regularises the controls, damps chattering, and keeps the QP well conditioned.

The cost specifies the grasp on both axes: where to touch (drive \(\mathbf{x}_k^{i}\) to \(\mathbf{x}_\star^{i}\)) and how hard to squeeze (drive \(f_k^{i}\) to \(\alpha_i\)), while the \(R\)-cost keeps the effort modest. The face pairing is fixed by the contact pairs and enforced through complementarity; the position term selects the precise point on each face.

§3

The Target Forces α from Force Closure

The targets \(\alpha_I, \alpha_M, \alpha_T\) are not free. They are the internal-force magnitudes that produce a net-zero wrench on the cube — a pure squeeze that holds without accelerating it. With the grasp matrix \(G\) of §0, the squeeze lives in its nullspace:

\[ G\,\mathbf{f}_{\text{internal}} = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \mathbf{f}_{\text{internal}} = \bigl(f^{I}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_I,\ f^{M}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_M,\ f^{T}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_T\bigr). \]

For index+middle (on \(-Y\)) opposing the thumb (on \(+Y\)), force balance along the squeeze axis gives the relation already encoded in GetGraspMagnitudes (cube_kinematics.h):

\[ \alpha_T = \alpha_I + \alpha_M, \qquad \alpha_M = \rho\,\alpha_I, \qquad \rho = \frac{a - c}{c - b}, \]

where \(a, b\) are the index/middle offsets on the \(-Y\) face and \(c\) the thumb offset on \(+Y\). One free scalar (the overall squeeze magnitude \(\alpha_I\)) is chosen large enough that friction resists gravity — \(\mu\,\alpha \gtrsim m g\) — and the remaining two targets follow. That gravity is carried by the tangential (friction) force, not the normal force, is exactly why the friction components of §4 must be modelled and not assumed (§5, Case 2).

§4

Contact & Friction — the Stewart–Trinkle Cone

Each finger's contact is modelled exactly as C3 does (§1 of the solver document): a normal force, a polyhedral (pyramid) approximation of the friction cone with explicit tangential weights, and a sliding slack — all coupled by three complementarity relations.

The friction cone and its polyhedral approximation

The exact Coulomb condition couples the tangential force magnitude to the normal force:

\[ \bigl\|\mathbf{f}_{t,k}^{i}\bigr\| \;\le\; \mu\, f_k^{i}, \qquad f_k^{i} \ge 0 \quad\text{(can only push)}. \]

C3 replaces the circular cone by an \(n_d\)-sided pyramid so the constraint is linear. The tangential force is a nonnegative combination of fixed direction vectors \(\mathbf{d}_j^{i}\) spanning the tangent plane, and the cone bound becomes a single linear "friction budget":

\[ \mathbf{f}_{t,k}^{i} = \sum_{j=1}^{n_d} \beta_{k,j}^{i}\,\mathbf{d}_j^{i} = D^{i}\boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i} \ge \mathbf{0}, \qquad \mathbf{e}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i} \;\le\; \mu\, f_k^{i}, \]

where \(\mathbf{e} = (1,\dots,1)^\top\). The slack in this budget, \(\mu f_k^{i} - \mathbf{e}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i} \ge 0\), is "remaining friction capacity"; it is the quantity complementary to sliding below.

The three complementarity relations per contact

Let \(\phi_k^{i} = \phi^{i}(\mathbf{q}_k)\) be the signed gap between tip \(i\) and its cube face, \(J_t^{i}\) the tangential contact Jacobian, and \(\gamma_k^{i}\) the sliding speed. The Stewart–Trinkle conditions are:

\(0 \le f_k^{i} \;\perp\; \phi_k^{i} \ge 0\)   — normal: push only when touching

\(0 \le \boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i} \;\perp\; \bigl(\gamma_k^{i}\mathbf{e} + \Delta t\,J_t^{i}\dot{\mathbf{q}}_k\bigr) \ge 0\)   — tangential: friction opposes the sliding direction

\(0 \le \gamma_k^{i} \;\perp\; \bigl(\mu f_k^{i} - \mathbf{e}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i}\bigr) \ge 0\)   — budget: slide only when friction is saturated

Normal

Either the gap is zero (contact, force allowed) or the force is zero (separated).

Tangential

A friction weight \(\beta_{k,j}^{i}\) is active only in directions where the contact is actually sliding; \(\gamma_k^{i}\) lifts the relative velocity to be \(\ge 0\) in every pyramid direction.

Budget

The contact sticks (\(\gamma_k^{i}=0\)) while friction capacity remains; it begins to slide only once the budget \(\mu f_k^{i}-\mathbf{e}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i}\) hits zero.

Packaged into the LCS gap vector

Stacking the right-hand sides of the three relations for all three fingers gives the gap vector \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_k\), complementary to \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\), exactly the object the solver works with:

\[ \boldsymbol{\eta}_k = E\,\mathbf{x}_k + F\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + H\,\mathbf{u}_k + \mathbf{c}, \qquad 0 \;\le\; \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k \;\perp\; \boldsymbol{\eta}_k \;\ge\; 0. \]

Note the tangential row carries the fixed factor \(\Delta t\) (the timestep that scales velocity into displacement, \(E_t = \Delta t\) in the solver). Since \(\Delta t\) is a constant parameter, this row stays linear and the LCS is exactly C3's.

§5

Dynamics, Schedule & The Complete Problem

Dynamics — the linearised LCS

The nonlinear contact dynamics \(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = f(\mathbf{x}_k,\mathbf{u}_k)\) are linearised at the current operating point by Drake AutoDiff into the constant matrices \(A, B, D, \mathbf{d}\) (§3 of the solver document), with the fixed timestep \(\Delta t\) already folded in — exactly C3's form:

\[ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\,\mathbf{x}_k + B\,\mathbf{u}_k + D\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + \mathbf{d}, \qquad \forall k. \]

\(D\) is the bridge that turns the contact forces \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) into cube acceleration. Because nothing in the problem multiplies two decision variables, the dynamics remain affine and the LCS is untouched relative to the stock C3 controller.

Contact schedule and fingertip kinematics

The first 80 of 100 steps are a free-space approach with no contact; the squeeze is confined to the last 20 steps. Fingertip points are tied to the configuration by forward kinematics:

\[ \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^{i} = \mathbf{0}\ \ (k = 0,\dots,79), \qquad \mathbf{x}_k^{i} = \mathrm{FK}_i(\mathbf{q}_k). \]
Grasp-synthesis QPCC — fixed horizon, with friction cone

\(\displaystyle \min_{\substack{\mathbf{x}_{0:N},\ \mathbf{u}_{0:N-1} \\ \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0:N-1},\ \boldsymbol{\eta}_{0:N-1}}} \ \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Bigl[\, \mathbf{u}_k^\top R\,\mathbf{u}_k + \sum_{i}\bigl( w_p\|\mathbf{x}_k^{i}-\mathbf{x}_\star^{i}\|^2 + w_f(f_k^{i}-\alpha_i)^2 \bigr) \,\Bigr]\)

\(\text{subject to, for all } k \text{ and } i\in\{I,M,T\}:\)

\(\quad \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{x}_k + B\mathbf{u}_k + D\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + \mathbf{d} \qquad\textbf{[dynamics]}\)

\(\quad \boldsymbol{\eta}_k = E\mathbf{x}_k + F\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + H\mathbf{u}_k + \mathbf{c} \qquad\textbf{[gap definition]}\)

\(\quad 0 \le \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k \perp \boldsymbol{\eta}_k \ge 0 \qquad\textbf{[normal + tangential + budget complementarity]}\)

\(\quad \mathbf{e}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i} \le \mu f_k^{i},\quad \boldsymbol{\beta}_k^{i} \ge \mathbf{0},\quad f_k^{i}\ge 0 \qquad\textbf{[friction pyramid]}\)

\(\quad \mathbf{x}_k^{i} = \mathrm{FK}_i(\mathbf{q}_k) \qquad\textbf{[fingertip kinematics]}\)

\(\quad \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^{i} = \mathbf{0},\quad k = 0,\dots,79 \qquad\textbf{[contact schedule]}\)

\(\quad \mathbf{x}_0 = \hat{\mathbf{x}},\quad \alpha_T = \alpha_I + \alpha_M,\quad \alpha_M = \rho\,\alpha_I \qquad\textbf{[initial state + force-closure targets]}\)

This is a Quadratic Program with Complementarity Constraints over the friction-pyramid-augmented \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) — the same problem class and the same LCS as the Push-Anything controller, differing only in the cost (position + force tracking plus the \(R\)-cost) and the explicit contact schedule.

§6

How It Is Solved — The C3 Mapping

The complementarity constraint is non-convex, so the problem is solved by ADMM exactly as in §4–§8 of the solver document. The friction-pyramid variables \((\boldsymbol{\beta},\gamma)\) are already part of \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) there, so they need no special handling. The split is:

z-update (QP)

A convex QP over \((\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\eta})\) carrying the cost (now including the \(\mathbf{u}_k^\top R\,\mathbf{u}_k\) term), the linear dynamics, the gap equation, the friction-budget inequality, and the schedule \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k=\mathbf{0}\) for \(k<80\). Solved with OSQP.

δ-update (projection)

Projects onto \(0\le\boldsymbol{\lambda}\perp\boldsymbol{\eta}\ge0\) per step — the non-convex piece, now including the tangential and budget blocks. Reconciled with the QP by the dual \(\mathbf{w}\) until consensus.

With \(\Delta t\) fixed, the problem stays a pure QPCC — the inner solve is exactly the stock C3 z/δ/w iteration, unchanged. The only additions are the \(R\)-cost and the tracking terms in the z-update QP. The LCS matrices \(A,B,D,E,F,H,\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d}\) are rebuilt by AutoDiff at each re-linearisation, since \(G\), the gaps, and the contact Jacobians all move as the cube and fingers move (critique 4).

§7

Refinements & Cautions

The formulation above is faithful to the proposal. Three precise points make it well posed and solvable.

1

The pyramid is an inner approximation of the true cone

\(\mathbf{e}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta} \le \mu f_n\) with \(n_d\) edges under-approximates the circular cone \(\|\mathbf{f}_t\|\le\mu f_n\): the realisable friction is slightly direction-dependent and never exceeds the true cone. Use \(n_d = 4\) (fast, coarse) up to \(8\) (smoother, more variables) per contact. If you need the exact cone, the second-order-cone form is available but turns the inner solve into an SOCP rather than a QP — usually not worth it here.

2

The 80/20 schedule pre-empts what C3 is good at

Forcing \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k = \mathbf{0}\) for \(k < 80\) hands the optimizer a fixed contact time. That safe choice does solve "the fingers arrive together" — all three are released into contact at the same \(k\). But contact-implicit MPC's whole advantage is discovering contact timing through complementarity. If grasps fail because 20 steps is too few to build \(\alpha\), relax to a soft schedule (allow force after step 80, do not forbid it earlier) rather than hard-zeroing.

3

\(\mathbf{x}_k^{i}\) carries a cost now — the FK equality is mandatory

With the position-tracking term, the fingertip points are not decorative: the cost pulls \(\mathbf{x}_k^{i}\) toward \(\mathbf{x}_\star^{i}\). The equality \(\mathbf{x}_k^{i} = \mathrm{FK}_i(\mathbf{q}_k)\) must be enforced as a hard constraint — otherwise the optimizer satisfies the cheap position cost by floating the contact point to the target in mid-air while \(\mathbf{q}_k\) stays put. Since \(\mathbf{x}_k^{i}\) is a deterministic function of \(\mathbf{q}_k\), the cleanest formulation substitutes \(\mathrm{FK}_i(\mathbf{q}_k)\) directly into the cost (and into the gaps \(\phi^i\) and grasp matrix \(G\)), eliminating \(\mathbf{x}_k^{i}\) as a variable. \(\mathrm{FK}_i\) is nonlinear, so the position term is a nonlinear least-squares cost — linearise it at each re-linearisation alongside the LCS matrices, consistent with §6.

Bottom line

The structure is sound and maps cleanly onto the existing C3 stack — the LCS and the fixed timestep \(\Delta t\) are exactly C3's. It is the Push-Anything QPCC with (i) a position + force tracking cost plus the standard \(\mathbf{u}^\top R\,\mathbf{u}\) control cost, and (ii) explicit Stewart–Trinkle friction-pyramid variables and constraints. Pick a pyramid resolution (item 1), and enforce the schedule and FK equality (items 2–3), and the problem is well posed.

§8

Implementation Status, Problems & the Phase 1 + Phase 2 Pivot

This section records what was actually built, the problems encountered (both the solver issues we fixed and the one fundamental wall we hit), and the recommended change of approach.

8.1  ·  What we built

Current approach

We forked the c3 solver and added a force-reference cost \(\sum_k (\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k - \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^\star)^\top W (\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k - \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^\star)\) (methods SetForceTrackingWeight / UpdateForceTarget), weighting only the three normal-force entries so the squeeze magnitudes track \(\alpha_I,\alpha_M,\alpha_T\). A test program (allegro_grasp_c3.cc) builds the Allegro+cube plant, places the fingertips on the cube via IK, linearizes to an LCS, and runs one C3 solve with a heavy cube-pose anchor \(Q\) plus this force cost. The intent: command \(\alpha_M\) and read back the planned normal forces, then sweep \(\alpha_M\) to find the squeeze that lets friction resist gravity.

8.2  ·  Numerical problems encountered (and fixed)

Getting a single solve to run took a sequence of fixes, all symptoms of an ill-conditioned, stiff MPC for a torque-controlled hand:

SymptomRoot causeFix
OSQP "non-convex" in the ADMM projection step The projection Hessian's \(\lambda\)-block is the LCS matrix \(F\), whose symmetric part is not PSD for a frictional contact Regularize the projection solver (\(\sigma = 1.0\), equilibration) in the c3 fork
Main QP IterationLimit → zero fallback \(\lvert B\rvert \approx 1638\): torque acting on light finger joints makes a hugely ill-scaled input matrix Input rescaling \(\tilde{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{u}/s_u\) so \(\lvert B\rvert\!\to\!\lvert A\rvert\); scale \(R\) by \(s_u^2\)
SolverSpecificError A hand-rolled OSQP option set dropped polishing/\(\rho\)/\(\alpha\) Restore C3's tuned default options; loosen eps to 1e-3
Stiff contact dynamics Fingertip collision spheres penetrated the cube by ~17 mm Back the fingertips off to a ~3 mm contact gap

After these, the solve converges and produces nonzero contact forces — the plant → LCS → C3 → force-cost pipeline works end to end.

8.3  ·  The fundamental wall: C3 cannot command the squeeze

!

The grip squeeze is an internal force, and C3 is blind to it

The squeeze is an internal force: equal-and-opposite presses from opposing fingers that cancel, producing zero net wrench on the cube. Formally it lives in the nullspace of the grasp map \(G\) (see the grasp-theory critique).

\[ G\,\mathbf{f}_{\text{squeeze}} = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{the cube's motion is identical for any squeeze magnitude.} \]

C3 chooses contact forces only by how they drive the state (the cube pose), which responds solely to the net wrench. Since the squeeze produces no net wrench, C3 is structurally blind to it — it has no reason to prefer any squeeze level. Our force-reference cost tries to pin it anyway, but it is a soft preference competing against the state anchor.

Confirmed empirically:

  • Raising the force-cost weight 100× barely moved the normal forces (they crept from \(-0.16\) toward \(-0.05\), i.e. toward the target by a sliver) — the cost is wired correctly but overwhelmingly dominated.
  • Removing the state anchor to let the force cost win instead broke solvability (the anchor was also providing the regularization that makes the QP solvable) → IterationLimit → zero.

So we are in a vise: the state anchor is required for the problem to solve, yet it dictates the forces and steamrolls the squeeze command. A state-tracking MPC cannot command an internal grip force — not a bug, but the nature of the tool.

8.4  ·  The pivot: a two-phase (hybrid position/force) controller

A grasp has two sub-tasks that need opposite kinds of control, and no single controller does both well:

Sub-taskNeedsGood at itBad at it
Bring all fingers to contact simultaneously (timing) planning ahead / coordination C3 / trajectory optimization force control (fingers arrive raggedly; the first to land shoves the cube)
Squeeze with force \(\alpha\) once in contact reacting to contact with a set force force / PD control (\(\boldsymbol{\tau}=J^\top\mathbf{f}\)) C3 (squeeze is invisible, §8.3)

The resolution is the classic hybrid position/force control split — divide the grasp in time:

Phase 1 — Reach (free space)

Use a coordinated trajectory (C3 / planning) to bring all three fingertips to the cube surface arriving at the same instant. Position/ trajectory controlled; no squeezing yet. This solves the timing problem.

Phase 2 — Squeeze (in contact)

The moment all three fingers report contact, switch to force control \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \mathbf{f}\) and command the squeeze \(\alpha\). This directly sets the grip force — the quantity C3 cannot.

The switch trigger is "all three contacts closed" (gap \(\approx 0\) / small measured force). Phase 1 covers the timing that force control is bad at; Phase 2 covers the squeeze that C3 is blind to. Each tool does only what it is good at, and the friction-vs- gravity question is then answered directly by sweeping \(\alpha\) in Phase 2 (or by simulating and observing whether the cube is held).

Status & recommendation

The C3 force-reference machinery is built and the solve works, but §8.3 shows it cannot serve as the squeeze command. Recommended next step: keep C3 (or a simpler coordinated plan) for Phase 1, and build the \(\boldsymbol{\tau}=J^\top\mathbf{f}\) Phase 2 squeeze controller with a contact-triggered handoff between them.

§9

Hybrid Controller — Exact Implementation Steps

A concrete build plan for the Phase 1 + Phase 2 controller of §8.4. It is built on top of allegro_cube_visualizer.cc, which already has the Allegro+cube plant, the Meshcat visualization, the simulation loop, and a PD joint controller. We add one controller system with two modes and a contact-triggered switch.

9.0  ·  Architecture

One system, two modes

A new HybridGraspController (a Drake LeafSystem): input port = Allegro state \((\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\); output port = joint torques. It holds a discrete mode (kReach / kSqueeze) and per-finger contact flags. Each control step it computes the contact gaps, updates the mode, and emits torques from whichever phase is active. The cube pose is read from the plant context (sim) so gaps and contact normals are known exactly.

9.1  ·  Phase 1 — coordinated reach (position control)

Goal: bring all three fingertips to the cube surface without shoving the cube.

  1. Contact configuration. Compute \(\mathbf{q}_{\text{contact}}\) with SolveGraspIK (cube_kinematics.h), targeting the fingertips exactly at each face — GetGraspPositions(X_WC, cube_size, a, b, c), no inward penetration. (Penetration is Phase 2's job; targeting the surface means an early-arriving finger rests with ~0 force and does not push the cube.)
  2. Pre-grasp configuration. Compute \(\mathbf{q}_{\text{pregrasp}}\) with the fingertips offset a few cm outside the faces (GetIntermediatePosition).
  3. Synchronized trajectory. Build one cubic spline \(\mathbf{q}_{\text{ref}}(t)\): open \(\to \mathbf{q}_{\text{pregrasp}} \to \mathbf{q}_{\text{contact}}\), with a single common end time, using PiecewisePolynomial::CubicShapePreserving (as the visualizer already does). All fingers are scheduled to reach the surface together.
  4. Track it with the existing PdJointController law \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = K_p(\mathbf{q}_{\text{ref}}-\mathbf{q}) + K_d(\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{ref}}-\dot{\mathbf{q}})\), plus gravity compensation (9.3, step 4) so the fingers do not sag.
Why this fixes the timing

Targets are at the surface, so contact never produces a shove — fingers that arrive early simply rest there. The Phase-2 switch (9.2) waits until all three are touching, so residual timing differences are absorbed by waiting rather than requiring perfect simultaneity.

9.2  ·  The handoff trigger (contact detection)

  1. Per-finger gap by FK. Each step, get the fingertip world position \(\mathbf{p}_i = \) plant.EvalBodyPoseInWorld(ctx, tip_i).translation() and the cube pose \(X_{WC}\). The signed gap to finger \(i\)'s face is \(\phi_i = (\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}^{\text{face}}_i)\cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}_i\), where \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_i\) is the inward face normal and \(\mathbf{p}^{\text{face}}_i\) the face point.
  2. Contact flag. Finger \(i\) is "in contact" when \(\phi_i \le \varepsilon\) (e.g. \(\varepsilon = 1\) mm). (Alternatively read normal force from plant.get_contact_results_output_port().)
  3. Switch. When all three flags are true for a few consecutive steps (debounce), set mode \(\to\) kSqueeze and latch it.

9.3  ·  Phase 2 — squeeze (force control, \(\boldsymbol{\tau}=J^\top\mathbf{f}\))

  1. Squeeze magnitudes. From GetGraspMagnitudes: with offsets \(\rho=1\), \(\alpha_I=\alpha_M=\) --alpha_m, \(\alpha_T=\alpha_I+\alpha_M\).
  2. Desired contact forces (world frame), pointing inward along each face normal:
    \[ \mathbf{f}_I = \alpha_I\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}_I,\quad \mathbf{f}_M = \alpha_M\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}_M,\quad \mathbf{f}_T = \alpha_T\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}_T \]
    For the axis-aligned cube: \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_I=\hat{\mathbf{n}}_M=+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) (−Y face), \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_T=-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) (+Y face). If the cube rotates, transform the cube-frame normals by \(X_{WC}\).
  3. Map forces to torques. For each finger get the fingertip translational Jacobian and accumulate:
    \[ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{squeeze}} = \sum_{i\in\{I,M,T\}} J_i(\mathbf{q})^\top \mathbf{f}_i \]
    via plant.CalcJacobianTranslationalVelocity(ctx, kV, tip_i, [0,0,0], world, world, &Ji) (a \(3\times n_v\) matrix; \(J_i^\top \mathbf{f}_i\) gives an \(n_v\) torque vector).
  4. Gravity compensation. Add \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_g = -\,\)plant.CalcGravityGeneralizedForces(ctx) so the hand holds its pose against gravity.
  5. Total + ramp. \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{squeeze}} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_g\), selecting the Allegro rows for the actuation port. Ramp \(\alpha\) from 0 to target over ~0.5 s on entering kSqueeze for a smooth grip.
Note — friction is what holds gravity

The commanded \(\mathbf{f}_i\) are horizontal squeeze (normal) forces; they do not directly resist gravity. The cube is held by friction at the contacts, bounded by \(\mu\,\alpha\). The whole point of sweeping \(\alpha\) is to find where \(\mu\cdot(\text{total normal force}) \gtrsim m g\) (analytically \(\alpha_M \approx 0.6\) N for \(m=0.2\) kg, \(\mu=0.8\)).

  2. Replace the PD-only controller with HybridGraspController. Connect plant.get_state_output_port(allegro_index) → controller.state and controller.torques → plant.get_actuation_input_port(allegro_index).
  3. Give the controller the plant + a context handle (or pass the cube pose in) so it can evaluate FK, gaps, Jacobians, and gravity each step.
  4. Expose --alpha_m, \(K_p,K_d\), the contact threshold \(\varepsilon\), and the squeeze ramp time as gflags.

  1. Run with Meshcat open. Watch Phase 1: all three fingers reach the cube together and rest.
  2. Watch the switch to Phase 2 and the grip ramp in.
  3. Sweep --alpha_m (e.g. 0.3 → 2.0 N). For each, observe whether the cube is held or slips out under gravity. The smallest \(\alpha_m\) that holds is the empirical friction threshold — compare to the analytic \(\approx 0.6\) N.
  4. Optional: enable the contact-force visualizer to confirm the normal forces match \(\alpha\).
Deliverable

One new file (e.g. allegro_grasp_hybrid.cc) + a HybridGraspController system + a BUILD target. Phase 1 reuses the existing IK / spline / PD pieces; Phase 2 is the \(J^\top\mathbf{f}\) + gravity-compensation law. The contact-triggered switch ties them together. This finally makes \(\alpha_m\) a direct, sweepable grip command — the thing C3 could not provide.

§10

As-Built Hybrid Controller — Behaviour & the Force-Tracking Gap

§9 was the plan; this section records what was actually built in examples/sampling_c3/allegro_grasp_hybrid.cc, how it behaves when run, and the one structural reason it does not yet hold the cube: nothing closes a loop on the force actually delivered at the grasp points.

10.1  ·  What was actually built (vs. the §9 plan)

The controller is not a wired LeafSystem; it is an explicit 1 kHz control loop inside DoMain that mutates the actuation input port each step via FixValue. Several deviations from §9 were forced by numerical reality:

  • Cube held by kinematic pinning, not a support wrench. Each step the cube is teleported back to its reference pose (positions + velocities reset) during reach and the early grip, then released. This is a perfect weld with none of the stiffness blow-up of an explicit support spring on the light, free cube.
  • Per-finger freeze-on-contact. When a finger first registers contact (above a force threshold, and only after contact_enable_t), its joints are latched into q_freeze[i] and held there while the others keep reaching.
  • Pre-grasp / contact IK + static preview. Two IK solves (fingertips 1 cm outside the faces, then 5 mm inside) define the reach spline waypoints. A pause-and-publish preview lets the operator verify the poses in Meshcat before running.
  • Phase 2 = posture hold + \(J^\top\mathbf{f}\). The squeeze law was augmented: alongside \(\boldsymbol{\tau}=J^\top\mathbf{f}\) it now re-uses the reach gains \((K_p,K_d)\) to hold each finger at its latched q_freeze pose, so the fingertips stay on the faces while the grip rides on top.

10.2  ·  Observed behaviour, in order

What works

With the reach gains raised, all three fingertips do reach the grasp points (tip error \(\sim\)1 cm) and freeze in a valid force-closure pose. The \(J^\top\mathbf{f}\) map is verified inward on all three fingers (alignment \(\approx 0.9\)–\(0.98\) with the line to the cube centre), and \(\alpha_m\) is a direct, sweepable grip command — the thing C3 could never give.

What breaks

The fingers jitter during the reach (high-gain PD on the light distal joints). After the switch the grip is applied but the fingertips slowly drift off the faces — tip error climbs from \(\sim\)1 cm to \(>\)12 cm. When the cube is unpinned it is no longer gripped, so it falls and the grasp is lost.

10.3  ·  The core gap — the applied force is never tracked

Every term in the controller is open-loop in force. The \(J^\top\mathbf{f}\) law converts a commanded fingertip force \(\mathbf{f}=\alpha\,\hat{\mathbf n}\) into joint torques, but nothing ever measures the actual normal force at the contact and corrects toward \(\alpha\). There is no error signal \(\big(\alpha - \lambda_{\text{n,measured}}\big)\) anywhere in the loop. Consequently:

  • At the switch the fingertips are pressed 5 mm into the cube; the pinned cube pushes back with a reaction much larger than the commanded \(\alpha\) (1–2 N). The net force is outward, so the fingers retreat.
  • The posture hold pulls toward q_freeze, but q_freeze and the \(J^\top\mathbf{f}\) push are two independent objectives that fight near the surface; their equilibrium is a finger sitting off the face, not a regulated contact force at the face.
  • Because the true contact force is never observed, the loop cannot tell the difference between "gripping at \(\alpha\)" and "not touching at all" — and drifts into the latter.
Diagnosis

The hybrid controller solved timing (coordinated reach) and gave us a direct grip command \(\alpha_m\), but it does not regulate the grip force at the grasp points. Position hold and open-loop \(J^\top\mathbf{f}\) are not a substitute for feedback on \(\lambda_{\text{n}}\). This is the same quantity §3–§4 model as the contact impulse \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) — exactly what the C3 / Stewart–Trinkle contact model exposes and what a force-tracking formulation must close a loop on.

Where this points next

The missing piece is a force-tracking law at the grasp points: read the measured contact normal force (from ContactResults / the LCS contact impulse \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\)) and drive it to \(\alpha\), rather than commanding \(\alpha\) blind. Whether that loop is closed by an explicit force-feedback term on top of the hybrid controller, or by folding the squeeze back into C3's \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) with the force-reference cost (§8), is the next design decision. The next section will develop it.

§11

Closing the Loop in C3 Space — Let the Grip Force Emerge from "Don't Fall"

§10 showed the hybrid controller never closes a loop on the delivered force. The proposal here is to put the whole grasp-and-hold inside C3, but to stop trying to command the squeeze. Instead, require the cube to stay where it is under gravity and let C3 discover the contact forces that achieve it. The grip force becomes an emergent output of "the cube must not fall," not an input we dial.

11.1  ·  The reframing: require equilibrium, read off the force

Everything so far pinned the cube during the grip and only released it at the end — so during the solve the cube never actually needed holding, and the squeeze was free-floating. Remove the pin. Let gravity act on the cube inside the C3 dynamics, and put a state cost that anchors the cube's world pose at its reference. Now the optimiser cannot leave the cube unsupported: the only thing that can cancel \(mg\) is contact friction, and friction is produced only at the fingertips.

11.2  ·  Why this escapes the §8.3 nullspace trap

§8.3 is still true: the internal squeeze produces zero net wrench on the cube, so by itself it is invisible to a state cost. What changes is the friction cone coupling. To hold the cube, the tangential (friction) impulses must supply the weight,

\( \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_{\text{t}}^{i} \;=\; mg \),   subject to   \( \lVert \lambda_{\text{t}}^{i}\rVert \le \mu\,\lambda_{\text{n}}^{i} \).

Combining the two gives a hard lower bound on the normal force: \( \sum_i \mu\,\lambda_{\text{n}}^{i} \ge mg \). The squeeze is no longer pure-nullspace — gravity makes a portion of it observable as the minimum normal force needed to keep friction inside its cone. That minimum is exactly the friction-vs-\(mg\) threshold we set out to find. C3, which already models the Stewart–Trinkle cone (§4) and the cube dynamics (§5), has every term needed to enforce this.

The residual freedom is real — and that is what the force cost is for

Only the lower bound on \(\lambda_{\text{n}}\) is pinned by gravity. Any squeeze above that bound still adds zero net wrench and is invisible to the state cost — the same nullspace as before. So C3 needs a tie-breaker to choose a unique grip. That is precisely the role of the force-reference cost already built into the fork (§8): \(W_\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\text{des}}\rVert^2\) on the normal entries. Set \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\text{des}}\) to a target slightly above the threshold (a safety margin) with a small \(W_\lambda\) — enough to regularise the nullspace, not so much that it overrides the equilibrium requirement.

11.3  ·  The formulation, concretely

Decision objects (per knot)
  • \(\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\): hand and free cube, cube unpinned.
  • \(\mathbf{u}\): joint torques (input-scaled, §“\(|B|\) blow-up” fix).
  • \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\): Stewart–Trinkle impulses at the 3 fingertip–cube contacts — \(\gamma_i,\ \lambda_{\text{n}}^{i},\ \lambda_{\text{t}}^{i}\).
Cost terms
  • \(Q\): anchor the cube’s world pose (position + orientation) at reference — this is what forbids "tracked while falling."
  • \(R\): quadratic on \(\mathbf{u}\) (no minimum-time term).
  • \(W_\lambda\): small force reference on the normal impulses (the tie-breaker / margin).

Dynamics and complementarity are the standard LCS (§5–§6), with two non-negotiables: (a) gravity on the cube is in the affine drift \(\mathbf d\); and (b) the cube anchor in \(Q\) is in the world frame, never cube-relative. The LCS is re-linearised each MPC step about the current contact configuration so the friction cone is evaluated where the fingers actually are.

How is the \(D\) matrix actually built when we form the LCS?

\(D\) is the one block of the LCS (§5) that §5 never writes out — and, unlike \(A,B,\mathbf d\), it is not produced by AutoDiff. It is assembled in closed form from the contact Jacobians and the mass matrix. The reason is worth being explicit about, because \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) is a complementarity variable, not a state — so there is no gradient tape on which to "track" it (see the call-out below).

Start from the semi-implicit time-stepping of the coupled hand-plus-cube dynamics, with the contact force \(\boldsymbol\lambda_k=(\gamma_k,\mathbf f_k,\boldsymbol\beta_k)\) entering as an exogenous applied force — injected through the contact Jacobians, never solved for inside this equation:

\[ M(\mathbf q_k)\,\bigl(\dot{\mathbf q}_{k+1}-\dot{\mathbf q}_k\bigr) \;=\; \Delta t\,\Bigl(\boldsymbol\tau_k^{\text{app}} \;+\; \underbrace{J_n^\top \mathbf f_k \;+\; J_t^\top \boldsymbol\beta_k}_{\text{contact force}}\Bigr). \]

\(M\) is the joint-space mass matrix of hand and free cube; \(J_n,J_t\) are the stacked normal / tangential contact Jacobians (the tangential rows already carry the §4 friction-pyramid directions, so the pyramid matrix \(D^{\!\beta}\) is folded into \(J_t\)). \(\boldsymbol\tau_k^{\text{app}}\) gathers the applied joint torques, Coriolis and gravity — and its cube-gravity piece is exactly what lands in the affine drift \(\mathbf d\) of 11.3, item (a). Solve for the next velocity and integrate the configuration forward through the velocity map \(N(\mathbf q)\):

\[ \dot{\mathbf q}_{k+1} = \dot{\mathbf q}_k^{\text{free}} + \Delta t\,M^{-1}\bigl(J_n^\top \mathbf f_k + J_t^\top \boldsymbol\beta_k\bigr), \qquad \mathbf q_{k+1} = \mathbf q_k + \Delta t\,N(\mathbf q_k)\,\dot{\mathbf q}_{k+1}. \]

The coefficient of \(\boldsymbol\lambda_k\) in \(\mathbf x_{k+1}=(\mathbf q_{k+1},\dot{\mathbf q}_{k+1})\) is \(D\). Only \(\mathbf f\) and \(\boldsymbol\beta\) carry force; the sliding slack \(\gamma\) never enters the dynamics, so its columns are structurally zero:

\[ D = \begin{bmatrix} \Delta t^{2}\,N(\mathbf q)\,M^{-1}\,\bar J^\top \\[4pt] \Delta t\,M^{-1}\,\bar J^\top \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \bar J^\top = \Bigl[\; \underbrace{\mathbf 0}_{\gamma}\;\big|\; \underbrace{J_n^\top}_{\mathbf f}\;\big|\; \underbrace{J_t^\top}_{\boldsymbol\beta}\;\Bigr]. \]

Reading the structure off directly:

  • The bottom block \(\Delta t\,M^{-1}\bar J^\top\) is the velocity update; the top block is the position update — the same thing carried one step further through \(\Delta t\,N(\mathbf q)\), the velocity-to-\(\dot{\mathbf q}\) map.
  • The \(M^{-1}\) is precisely why the tiny finger inertias wreck the conditioning (the \(|B|\sim10^3\) caveat in 11.5): a unit force on a light fingertip produces a large \(\Delta\dot{\mathbf q}\), so \(D\) and \(B\) share the same scaling fix \(s_u\).
  • The \(\gamma\) columns are zero in \(D\) by construction — the slack lives only in the gap vector \(\boldsymbol\eta\) (the \(E,F\) rows of §4), never in the dynamics.
What \(D\) does when there is no contact

\(D\) is purely geometric. The contact evaluators (ComputeContactJacobian) still return a positive gap \(\phi_i>0\) and a well-defined witness-point Jacobian even when a fingertip is nowhere near the cube, so the columns of \(D=\Delta t\,M^{-1}\bar J^\top\) stay populated — \(D\) has no idea whether the contact is live. What switches the force off is the complementarity, not \(D\): the normal row carries that positive gap,

\(0 \le \mathbf f_k \;\perp\; \bigl(\phi_k + \dots\bigr) \ge 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \phi_k>0 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf f_k=\mathbf 0,\)

and the empty friction budget \(\mu\mathbf f_k=\mathbf 0\) then forces \(\boldsymbol\beta_k=\mathbf 0\). With \(\boldsymbol\lambda_k=\mathbf 0\) the product \(D\boldsymbol\lambda_k\) vanishes and the knot collapses to the contact-free dynamics \(\mathbf x_{k+1}=A\mathbf x_k+B\mathbf u_k+\mathbf d\) — exactly the \(A,B,\mathbf d\) that AutoDiff produced. For the first 80 free-space knots the §5 contact schedule makes this explicit, hard-pinning \(\boldsymbol\lambda_k=\mathbf 0\) so those complementarity rows are dropped from the solve entirely.

The catch is conditioning: while the fingers are separated every contact row sits on its inactive branch \((\mathbf f=\mathbf 0,\ \phi>0)\), so no force couples into the state and C3 has almost no gradient by which to discover contact — the ill-conditioned "no active contact" regime flagged in §12. That is the argument for the pinned-reach handoff: enter C3 only once all three fingertips satisfy \(\phi_i\approx0\), so the very first LCS already has its contact rows active and the friction cone live.

Why \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) is never AutoDiffed

Because \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) is a complementarity variable, not a state, it is never seeded as an AutoDiff variable, and \(D\) is not a \(\partial\mathbf x_{k+1}/\partial\boldsymbol\lambda\) gradient. In the fork (LCSFactory::GenerateLCS) AutoDiff is seeded only on \((\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q},\mathbf u)\) (InitializeAutoDiff(q_v_u)) and applied to the contact-free acceleration \(\mathbf f = M^{-1}(\boldsymbol\tau_g+\boldsymbol\tau_u+\mathbf f_{\text{app}}-C)\); its gradient gives \(A,B,\mathbf d\). \(D\) is then built by hand — M.ldlt().solve(Jn.transpose()) and the \(J_t\) analogue (with \(M\) taken at its operating-point value via ExtractValue(M)), scaled by \(\Delta t\) and \(\Delta t^{2}N(\mathbf q)\). No linearisation is needed for the \(\boldsymbol\lambda\)-block because the force-to-state map is already exactly affine in \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) — which is precisely what makes this a Linear complementarity system. Re-linearising each MPC step (11.3) just re-evaluates \(J_n,J_t,M\) at the new finger / cube configuration.

11.4  ·  What this buys

Force is regulated, not commanded

If the cube starts to slip, the world-pose error grows and C3 responds by raising finger torque → more normal force → more friction → the cube is recaptured. The loop closes on the thing we actually care about (the cube staying put), with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) the internal variable doing the work.

The threshold is computed, not swept

With \(W_\lambda\) small, C3’s \(R\)-cost drives the grip toward the minimum \(\lambda_{\text{n}}\) that keeps friction in its cone — i.e. it solves for the \(\alpha_M\!\approx\!0.6\)–1 N threshold directly, instead of us hand-sweeping it.

Risks & required care
  • World-frame anchor is mandatory. A cube-relative grasp cost lets C3 hold the relative geometry while the cube falls (the §8 "tracked while falling" critique).
  • Conditioning. The tiny finger inertias gave \(|B|\sim10^3\); reuse the input scaling \(s_u\) and \(R\!\to\!s_u^2R\) already derived.
  • Projection convexity. The Stewart–Trinkle \(F\) is not PSD; keep the C3QP OSQP regularisation (sigma/scaling) from the fork.
  • The squeeze can still wander if \(W_\lambda=0\). It must be nonzero to pin the nullspace — the open question is how small it can be before the grip drifts, and how large before it overrides the gravity-driven minimum.
  • Real-time. Full hand+cube LCS with re-linearisation each step is heavier than the hybrid loop; the ADMM iteration budget needs checking at control rate.
First experiment to settle the question

Before committing the full MPC, run the single-solve test (the existing allegro_grasp_c3.cc harness) with the cube unpinned and under gravity, a world-frame cube anchor in \(Q\), and \(W_\lambda=0\). Read the converged \(\lambda_{\text{n}}^{i}\). If they sit at \(\approx mg/\mu\) and the cube holds, the gravity-driven lower bound works and C3-space is viable. If \(\lambda_{\text{n}}\) collapses to zero (cube falls in-solve), the equilibrium is not being enforced and we fall back to an explicit force-feedback term on the hybrid controller of §10.

§12

Pinned-Reach + C3-Squeeze — A Practical Two-Phase Architecture

§11 argued that releasing the kinematic pin and letting gravity act turns the squeeze force into something C3 can regulate. But the pin is also what makes the reach phase tractable: without it, C3 would have to simultaneously move the fingers and prevent the cube from tumbling before any contact has even been made. That is a much harder problem with a poorly-conditioned LCS (no active contacts → near-zero rows in the contact Jacobian).

The practical answer is to keep the two phases structurally separate. Let the existing PD-and-spline controller own the reach, with the cube pinned. Then, at the moment all three fingers have made contact, hand control off to C3 with the pin released. C3 only needs to solve the force-regulation problem — the hard geometric work (getting fingertips onto cube faces) is already done.

12.1  ·  The Two-Phase State Machine

Phase Controller Cube Exit condition
kPregrasp / kReach PD + cubic spline Kinematically pinned All 3 fingertips report \(\lambda_n^i > \epsilon\)
Handoff (one step) Latch \(\mathbf{q}_\text{freeze}\), build LCS Pin released — (instantaneous)
kSqueeze C3 (ADMM) Free, gravity acting Cube drops / experiment end

The pin release happens exactly at the handoff step, not gradually. At that instant, C3 sees a cube with nonzero gravitational drift — and must immediately find a contact force schedule that keeps it up. Because the fingers are already touching the cube faces (confirmed by the contact trigger), the contact Jacobian rows are populated and the LCS is well-posed from step one.

12.2  ·  What C3 Sees at Handoff — the LCS at the Contact Configuration

At the handoff instant, the full system state is \[ \mathbf{x}_0 = \bigl[\,\mathbf{q}_\text{hand},\; \mathbf{v}_\text{hand},\; \mathbf{q}_\text{cube},\; \mathbf{v}_\text{cube}\bigr] \in \mathbb{R}^{45} \] where \(\mathbf{q}_\text{hand}\approx\mathbf{q}_\text{freeze}\) (all 16 joints) and \(\mathbf{v} \approx 0\) (both hand and cube, since the reach was settling). The cube pose \(\mathbf{q}_\text{cube}=\mathbf{q}_\text{cube}^0\) is the reference pose (world-frame anchor target).

The LCS is built by linearizing Drake's continuous dynamics at \((\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{u}_0=\boldsymbol{\tau}_g)\) — the gravity-compensating torque — over one time step \(h\): \[ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\,\mathbf{x}_k + B\,\mathbf{u}_k + \mathbf{d} + D\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k, \quad \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k \ge 0, \; E\,\mathbf{x}_k + F\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + c \ge 0,\; \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^\top(\cdots)=0. \] The crucial terms:

  • \(\mathbf{d} = h\,M^{-1} \mathbf{f}_\text{grav}\) — gravity enters the drift, so the cube accelerates downward unless contact forces cancel it.
  • \(D\) — the contact-Jacobian transpose columns; one per finger, already populated because fingers are on the cube faces at \(\mathbf{x}_0\).
  • \(E, F, c\) — the complementarity rows encoding the Stewart–Trinkle cone, linearized at \(\mathbf{x}_0\). The gap function \(c_i \approx 0\) for each active contact (fingers are touching, not penetrating).
Why linearizing at contact is better-conditioned

During reach (no contact), the \(D\) columns for finger–cube contacts are zero — the fingers have not touched the cube yet. The LCS cannot express any contact force. Handing off to C3 only after contact means the very first C3 LCS already has all three \(D\) columns nonzero, the friction cone complementarity is active, and the solver immediately sees the full force–state coupling. The handoff also provides a natural warm-start: \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^* \approx mg/(3\mu)\) for each finger.

12.3  ·  Cost Matrices for the Squeeze Phase

Three terms drive the C3 objective in kSqueeze, in priority order:

Matrix What it penalises Role
Q \(\|\mathbf{q}_\text{cube} - \mathbf{q}_\text{cube}^0\|^2\) in world frame Keeps cube from falling. Forces friction lower bound \(\sum\mu\lambda_n \ge mg\).
R \(\|\mathbf{u} - \boldsymbol{\tau}_g\|^2\) Keeps joint torques near gravity comp. Prevents runaway squeeze.
Wλ \(\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|^2\) (all contact forces) Tie-breaker for the squeeze nullspace. Keep small — just enough to prevent drift above the gravity-driven lower bound.
Q must be world-frame — not cube-relative

If \(Q\) is expressed in the cube's own body frame, C3 can satisfy it while the cube translates downward (the relative geometry stays fixed). The cost must penalise the cube's world-frame position directly: \(Q = \text{blkdiag}(0_{16\times16},\, 0_{16\times16},\, Q_c,\, 0_{6\times6})\) where \(Q_c\) selects the translational part of the cube pose. This is the exact same issue flagged in §8.3 — any relative cost allows the "tracked while falling" failure.

12.4  ·  Keeping Fingers on the Cube Inside C3

The posture-hold term from the hybrid controller (§10) has a direct analogue in C3's state cost. Add a block to \(Q\) that penalises hand-joint deviation from \(\mathbf{q}_\text{freeze}\): \[ Q_\text{hand} = k_\text{hold}\,I_{16\times16} \] in the \(\mathbf{q}_\text{hand}\) sub-block of \(Q\). This keeps the fingers pressed against the cube faces while C3 finds the optimal torques, playing the same role as the \(k_p(\mathbf{q}_\text{freeze}-\mathbf{q}_\text{hand})\) term — but now inside the ADMM optimisation rather than bolted on outside it.

The value of \(k_\text{hold}\) should be in the same order as the existing PD gain (\(k_p = 10\)) translated to the cost scale of \(Q\). A sweep from \(k_\text{hold}=5\) to \(k_\text{hold}=50\) while watching \(\|\mathbf{q}_\text{hand}-\mathbf{q}_\text{freeze}\|\) in simulation is the fastest calibration path.

12.5  ·  Implementation Path

Step 1 — Single-solve diagnostic (§11.6, unchanged)

Run allegro_grasp_c3.cc with the fingers already at \(\mathbf{q}_\text{freeze}\), cube unpinned and under gravity, world-frame \(Q\), \(W_\lambda=0\). If the converged \(\lambda_n^i \approx mg/\mu \approx 1.2\,\text{N}\), the gravity-driven lower bound is working. This is the go/no-go gate.

Step 2 — Replace kSqueeze with C3 in the hybrid binary

In allegro_grasp_hybrid.cc, at the state-machine transition to kSqueeze:

  1. Release the cube pin (cube_pinned = false).
  2. Build the LCS by calling Drake's LinearizeContactDynamics (or the existing MakeLinearizedLCS helper in the c3 library) at \(\mathbf{x}_0 = (\mathbf{q}_\text{freeze},\,0,\,\mathbf{q}_\text{cube}^0,\,0)\).
  3. Construct \(Q\), \(R\), \(W_\lambda\) as above, pass to C3QP.
  4. Each sim step: call c3.Solve(x_current), extract \(\mathbf{u}^*[0]\), apply to the hand's actuation port.

The reach phase (kPregrasp / kReach) is untouched — the PD+spline controller that is already working continues to own those modes.

Step 3 — Re-linearise each step (optional, for longer horizons)

For a 5–10 step horizon the single linearization at \(\mathbf{x}_0\) may drift. Re-linearise at the current state each control cycle (iLQR-style). The added cost is one CalcMassMatrix + CalcJacobian call per finger per step — cheap relative to the ADMM solve itself.

12.6  ·  Residual Open Questions

What still needs to be decided
  • ADMM horizon \(N\). Longer is better for squeeze regulation but heavier per step. Start with \(N=3\) and check solve time at the simulator's control rate.
  • \(W_\lambda\) tuning. Too small → squeeze wanders in its nullspace. Too large → C3 minimises contact force and loses the grip. The §11 lower-bound argument says the minimum viable \(W_\lambda\) is "just large enough that OSQP doesn't see the nullspace as degenerate" — a value around \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-2}\) is a reasonable starting bracket.
  • Contact-frame convention. The C3 LCS expects \(\lambda = [\gamma, \lambda_n, \lambda_t]\) in its own contact frame. Verify that the contact normals extracted from Drake's proximity engine at the handoff instant agree with the \(\hat{n}_\text{cube}^i\) directions used in the hybrid Jᵀf computation.
  • Warm-start at each step. Initialise the ADMM with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda} = [0,\, mg/(3\mu),\, 0]\) for each finger. This is the exact gravity-equilibrium value and should cut the ADMM iteration count significantly.
§13

Lessons from Sampling-Based C3 (Push-Anything) — Why It Never Goes Unbounded

The §12 squeeze controller fails in a specific way: at the moment the kinematic pin is released, C3's z-update QP returns DualInfeasible (an unbounded-primal certificate), falls back to zero torque, and the cube drops. Yet Push-Anything — the franka_sampling_c3_controller / SamplingC3Controller stack — runs the same C3 machinery (the LCSFactory of §5, the ADMM solver of §6) on a planar push task and never hits this. This section is self-contained. Part A explains, in plain terms, exactly what the method does on every control tick — and answers two things that trip up first readers: where the "many trajectories" come from, and what C3 does when the robot is not touching the object. Part B distils six concrete reasons it stays robust, each pointed at where it lives in the source (systems/controllers/sampling_based_c3_controller.cc) and contrasted with the grasp controller.

Part A  —  How the method actually works

13.1  ·  The control loop, step by step

Push-Anything is ordinary C3 wrapped in a sampling-and-repositioning outer loop. C3 is the inner optimiser that plans a push; the outer loop decides where to push from. One full control tick:

  1. Read state. The robot end-effector (EE) position and the object's pose.
  2. Propose samples. A handful of candidate EE locations: the current EE spot, plus a few contact points scattered just outside the object's surface, plus the previous repositioning target. Call them \(K\) candidates.
  3. For each candidate, in parallel:
    • Build the LCS (§5), linearised with the EE at that candidate and the object at its current pose.
    • Solve C3 (ADMM, §6) → one planned trajectory: states \(\mathbf x_{0:N}\), inputs \(\mathbf u_{0:N-1}\), forces \(\boldsymbol\lambda\).
    • Roll it out: forward-simulate the regularised LCS from \(\mathbf x_0\) using the planned \(\mathbf u\) (optionally with an impedance controller tracking the planned EE path) → an honest "dynamically feasible" trajectory.
    • Score that rolled-out trajectory — mainly: how close did the object get to its goal.
  4. Pick the winner — the lowest-cost candidate.
  5. Choose a mode (with hysteresis, to avoid flip-flopping):
    • C3 mode — if pushing from the current EE is making progress, execute the winning C3 plan.
    • Repositioning mode — if a different candidate is much better, a better contact point is elsewhere: ignore C3 and move the EE there along a scripted, object-avoiding spline (not C3).
  6. Execute one step. Apply only the first input of the chosen plan (C3's \(\mathbf u_0\), or the repositioning trajectory's current command).
  7. Repeat from step 1 on the next tick.
Where does each rollout start from? — the sampled contact point itself

Each sample's rollout (and the C3 solve feeding it) starts from that sample's own initial state \(\mathbf x_0\), not from where the robot currently is. In code the rollout begins at x_plan[0], and C3 pins \(\mathbf x_{[0]}=\mathbf x_0=\) candidate_states[i]. That candidate state is the current measured state with exactly one thing changed: the EE position (the first three entries of \(\mathbf x\)) is overwritten by the sample's contact location; the object pose and all velocities stay at their current measured values (repositioning_target_state = x_lcs_curr; .head(3) = sample).

So for the current-location sample, \(\mathbf x_0\) is the real state. For every other sample, \(\mathbf x_0\) is a hypothetical teleport: the EE placed at that candidate contact point, the object left where it actually is. Each such sample therefore answers a what-if — "if the hand were already at this contact point, how well could it push the object from here?" — and scores the value of the location, not the motion of getting there. That is exactly why repositioning is a separate layer: if a teleported sample scores far better than the current spot, the controller knows a better contact point exists and physically drives the EE there — supplying the motion the sample skipped. (For the current sample, \(\mathbf x_0\) is additionally a short prediction ahead of the measured state, use_predicted_x0_c3, to cover the control-loop delay.)

So the contact-rich optimisation (C3) and the "where do I even put the hand" decision (sampling + repositioning) are two separate layers. That separation is the whole trick — and it is what the grasp controller is missing.

13.2  ·  "Where do the many trajectories come from? I thought C3 returns one."

Correct — and your MPC mental model is right: one C3 solve returns exactly one \((\mathbf x,\mathbf u)\) trajectory; you apply the first input, then re-solve next tick. Push-Anything does precisely that. The "many trajectories" are not several outputs of a single solve. They come from running C3 several times per tick — once per sample (step 3 above): \(K\) candidate EE locations → \(K\) separate LCS builds → \(K\) separate C3 solves → \(K\) candidate trajectories. The roll-out-and-score step is just the referee that picks which of the \(K\) candidates to use this tick. Once it picks, it is back to textbook MPC: execute the winner's first input, discard the rest, redo everything next tick.

Why re-simulate instead of trusting C3's own predicted cost?

C3's QP is a relaxation: it softens the hard contact (complementarity) constraints so it can solve fast. Its predicted trajectory can therefore be physically optimistic — it can "plan" a grip with a negative normal force that cannot exist (recall the grasp's \(\lambda_n=-1.5\) N). If you ranked candidates by C3's own predicted cost, you might crown one that only looks good because C3 cheated on the physics. So you push the planned inputs through the regularised LCS — an honest physics rollout — and score that. Now the comparison is fair, and a plan secretly relying on impossible forces scores badly and loses.

13.3  ·  What C3 does when the robot is not touching the object

When the EE is out of contact, every contact gap \(\phi_i>0\). Complementarity then forces \(\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0\) (positive gap ⇒ zero force — the §11 "no contact" box). The robot exerts no force on the object, so within the LCS the object is decoupled: it coasts on its own passive dynamics (on the table, it just sits still). What C3 plans then depends entirely on whether it can reach contact inside its short horizon — \(N=5\) steps at \(\Delta t\approx0.05\)–\(0.1\) s, i.e. only \(\sim0.25\)–\(0.5\) s of motion:

Contact reachable within the horizon

C3 does the thing contact-implicit MPC exists for: it co-plans the approach, the moment of contact, and the push in one shot. Complementarity lets \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) switch on at the later knots where the gap closes, and C3 chooses an EE motion that makes contact and then drives the object. This is strictly smarter than "path-plan, then track" — it optimises when to touch.

Contact NOT reachable (EE too far)

\(\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0\) at every knot, so the object-cost gradient w.r.t. \(\mathbf u\) is zero — nothing the robot can do in-horizon moves the object. C3 gets no useful signal about the object and does not invent a long-range path to it; it falls back to its robot-state cost (≈ stay put). This is the blind spot.

But what actually pulls the robot toward contact? — your worry is well-founded

You are right to doubt a smooth pull. While the gap \(\phi_i>0\), complementarity forces \(\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0\), the object is frozen, and the object-tracking cost is therefore flat with respect to any EE-approach motion — a plateau. A plain gradient step toward the object lowers nothing, so first-order reasoning genuinely says "nothing leads there." That is your intuition, and it is correct.

What can still find contact is the ADMM split of §6, not a gradient. C3 alternates between (i) a QP that treats \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) as a free variable — it will gladly set \(\lambda>0\) to move the object and cut the cost — and (ii) a projection that permits \(\lambda>0\) only where the gap is closed (\(0\le\lambda\perp\eta\)). The consensus between them pressures the plan to close the gap — move the EE in — so the force the QP wants becomes admissible. That coupling is what reaches toward contact; the contact Jacobian \(E=\partial\phi/\partial\mathbf x\) (nonzero even out of contact) is what carries EE motion into the gap row so the projection can eventually switch \(\lambda\) on.

And here your worry bites exactly: this only fires if some knot's gap can actually reach zero within the horizon — the EE must be within \(\sim\! N\Delta t\) of reach. If it is farther, no knot closes, the projection can never admit the wanted force, the consensus gives up, and the solution collapses to the trivial \(\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0\), EE-stays-put plateau. Then there is genuinely nothing pulling it to contact. So "reach + contact + push" is real, but only within roughly one horizon, and even there it is a fragile mode discovery rather than a gradient. Beyond that you are simply right — which is the whole reason Push-Anything teleports samples onto contact (13.1) and repositions, instead of asking C3 to find contact from afar.

So, directly to the question: it does not become a global path planner. Inside the horizon it is full contact-implicit planning (better than tracking); outside it, it goes inert on the object. Getting the EE from far to a contact point is deliberately not C3's job — that is the repositioning module, a scripted spline-plus-tracking, which is the "basic path planning and tracking" you intuited, living outside C3. For the grasp this is decisive: three fingers far from the cube give all-positive gaps, \(\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0\), no gradient — C3 cannot plan the reach. The §12 pinned-reach (PD + spline) is the grasp's "repositioning": it does the basic path-planning/tracking to seat the fingers, and only then is C3 within a useful contact horizon.

13.4  ·  Structural: the object is passively supported

The push-T contact set is (ee–ground, ee–T, T–ground) (resolve_contacts_to_lists: [[0,1,3],[0,2,3]]) on a planar workspace (\(z\in[-0.01,0.3]\)). Gravity on the object is carried by the table, not by the robot. When the pusher is out of contact the object simply sits still: the LCS/QP stays well-posed, no contact force is needed to keep it up, and there is nothing unstable to stabilise. The grasp has no such "table" — the cube is held only by the fingers, so losing contact turns the plant into an unstable free body that C3 must catch through a degenerate contact. This is the root asymmetry; every numerical safeguard below is what lets the well-posed case stay robust, but none of them manufacture a missing support.

13.5  ·  The C3 QP is box-constrained on inputs and states

The direct cause of — and fix for — DualInfeasible

Before each sample's solve the controller adds hard bounds (sampling_based_c3_controller.cc ≈ lines 996–1053): EE and object workspace limits and EE velocity limits as STATE constraints, and horizontal/vertical input limits \([-50,50]\) as INPUT constraints, all via AddLinearConstraint(...).

DualInfeasible is primal unboundedness. With \(\mathbf u\) and the state boxed, the feasible set's recession cone is trivial, so the QP can never be unbounded — regardless of how ill-conditioned \(D,B\) are or how degenerate the contact. The grasp squeeze C3 adds zero such constraints (only the dynamics equality and \(\mathbf x_0\) fix), so \(\mathbf u\) and \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) are free and OSQP chases an unbounded ray. (The squeeze code even has a tau_max clamp, but it is commented out — and a post-hoc clamp is weaker than a bound inside the QP.) Adding torque and finger-velocity bounds to the program is the single highest-leverage fix for the crash.

Concretely: the constraints to add to the grasp \(\mathbf u\)

In the grasp, \(\mathbf u\) is the 16 hand-joint torques, but C3 works in a scaled input \(\tilde{\mathbf u}\): the squeeze code scales \(B\!\to\!s_uB\) and applies \(\boldsymbol\tau = s_u\,\tilde{\mathbf u}\) (with \(s_u\!\approx\!0.1\) in the logs). So a physical torque limit must be divided by \(s_u\) before it becomes a bound on C3's variable. Per joint \(i\):

\( -\,\dfrac{\tau_{\max,i}}{s_u} \;\le\; \tilde u_i \;\le\; \dfrac{\tau_{\max,i}}{s_u} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad |\boldsymbol\tau_i|\le\tau_{\max,i}, \)

added as 16 INPUT box constraints. Alongside, two STATE boxes:

  • Finger joint-velocity limits \(|\dot q_i|\le\omega_{\max}\) — the analogue of Push-Anything's EE-velocity cap; stops the QP planning explosive finger motions and is what actually kills the velocity-direction unbounded rays.
  • Finger joint-position limits \(q_i\in[q_i^{-},q_i^{+}]\) from the Allegro URDF — keeps planned configurations physical.

\(\boldsymbol\lambda\) needs no explicit bound: complementarity (projection) plus the §11.5 force-reference \(W_\lambda\) already pin it. Boxing \(\tilde{\mathbf u}\) alone removes the unbounded ray; adding the velocity box makes it robust. Use the existing FLAGS_tau_max for \(\tau_{\max}\) — it is already defined, just unused.

13.6  ·  It never executes C3's plan — it scores a dynamically-feasible rollout

This is the code behind 13.2. CalcCost (sampling_based_c3_controller.cc ≈ lines 493–600) discards C3's own predicted states and re-simulates the LCS forward from the planned inputs (SimulateLCSOverTrajectory / SimulatePDControlWithLCS), then scores that trajectory. The push-T config uses kSimImpedanceObjectCostOnly — "simulate the EE as if tracked by an impedance controller, and score only whether the object moved toward the goal." Concretely for the grasp: instead of applying \(\boldsymbol\tau = s_u\,\texttt{GetInputSolution()}\) blindly, you would roll the planned torques through the regularised LCS, and if the cube falls in that rollout, fall back to the PD preload rather than executing a plan that only holds on paper.

13.7  ·  The evaluation contact solve is regularised — what that means, from scratch

To "roll out" (13.2/13.6) means: take the planned inputs \(\mathbf u\) and step the physics forward one tick at a time to see what actually happens. But stepping contact physics forward is not a plain matrix multiply — at every single step you first have to solve for the contact forces, and only then can you update the state.

Step 1 · Why finding contact forces is its own little solve

A contact force is not free to be anything. It obeys three "either/or" rules: a finger can only push (\(\lambda\ge0\), never pull); it pushes only when actually touching (gap \(=0\)); and if there is a gap (gap \(>0\)) the force must be zero. Packed together: "for each contact, either the gap is zero or the force is zero — never both positive." That either/or is the complementarity condition of §4, and finding the forces that satisfy it is a small problem called a Linear Complementarity Problem (LCP). Using the same gap vector \(\boldsymbol\eta\) of §4, one simulation step asks: given the current state \(\mathbf x\) and input \(\mathbf u\), find \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) with

\[ 0 \;\le\; \boldsymbol\lambda \;\perp\; \underbrace{F\boldsymbol\lambda + (E\mathbf x + H\mathbf u + \mathbf c)}_{\boldsymbol\eta} \;\ge\; 0 , \qquad\text{then}\qquad \mathbf x_{\text{next}} = A\mathbf x + B\mathbf u + D\boldsymbol\lambda + \mathbf d . \]

Here \(\mathbf q = E\mathbf x + H\mathbf u + \mathbf c\) is "how open each gap is right now," and \(F\) is "how each contact force pushes on each gap." The code solves exactly this with a classic pivoting routine (Drake's MobyLcpSolver, Lemke's algorithm) and then does the simple linear state update.

Step 2 · Why the raw LCP "misfires" for grasping

Lemke's algorithm is only guaranteed to find a clean, unique answer when \(F\) is a "nice" matrix (positive-definite-ish). For a frictional Stewart–Trinkle contact, \(F\) is not nice — its symmetric part is not positive semidefinite (the §6 / §11.5 fact). When \(F\) is not nice the LCP can have no solution, several solutions, or send the pivoting search off to infinity and return garbage. This bites hardest at exactly the configurations the grasp lives in: a fingertip barely touching (gap \(\approx0\), force \(\approx0\)), a force sitting right on the edge of the friction cone, the cube on the verge of slipping. There the LCP is on a knife-edge where a speck of numerical noise flips which contacts are "on," so the raw solve returns jumpy or nonsensical forces. And if the force at one step is garbage, the whole rolled-out trajectory is garbage, so the cost you read off it is garbage — which destroys the whole point of scoring samples by their rollout (13.2/13.6).

Step 3 · What "regularising" does to fix it

Regularising means: add a tiny positive number \(\varepsilon\) to the diagonal of \(F\) and solve with \(F+\varepsilon I\) instead of \(F\). That nudge makes the matrix positive-definite, which guarantees the LCP has exactly one solution and that Lemke's algorithm converges. Physically it is like giving each contact a hair of springiness instead of a perfectly rigid, knife-edge wall — the ambiguity disappears and the forces come out smooth and repeatable.

You want \(\varepsilon\) as small as possible so the physics is barely changed, so the "regularised Lemke" routine sweeps it: start at \(\varepsilon = 10^{\,\texttt{min\_exp}}\) and only grow it (×10 at a time) if the smaller value still fails. min_exp = -8 sets the floor at \(\varepsilon = 10^{-8}\) — the library default is \(10^{-20}\) ("try essentially no regularisation first"); Push-Anything raises the floor to \(10^{-8}\) because for these contact-rich configs a vanishingly small nudge never helps, so it skips straight to one that does. Net effect: the rollout always returns a well-defined, continuous contact force, so sample costs are stable and comparable even at the near-degenerate configurations.

Same disease, two places — the link to §11.5

The non-nice \(F\) hurts in two separate solves, and both are cured the same way — a small positive diagonal. On the planning side, inside C3's projection/QP, §11.5 says keep the OSQP \(\sigma\)/scaling regularisation. On the evaluation side, inside the rollout's LCP, this is the \(\varepsilon I\) above. One regularises the optimiser, the other regularises the simulator; identical idea, identical reason. Practical consequence for the grasp: the squeeze controller does no rollout today (it executes C3's raw plan), so it has no analogue — but the moment you add the rollout-gating of 13.6, you must use the regularised simulate, or your "honest" rollout will itself misfire at exactly the grazing-contact configs you care about, and the honest cost will not be honest.

13.8  ·  It uses the C3+ projection, not C3QP

Push-Anything sets projection_type: 'C3+' with qp_projection_alpha: 0.01 (instantiated as C3Plus). Per its options file: "as \(\alpha\to0\), any complementarity constraint error in QP projection \(\to 0\)." The grasp squeeze runs the plain C3QP. The \(F\)-not-PSD projection degeneracy of §6 is handled more robustly by C3+; it is a drop-in projection swap worth trying on the grasp.

13.9  ·  A failed solve is data, never a stop — sampling + repositioning

When a solve fails, Push-Anything still sets the same zero-input fallback the grasp hits — but here that just makes the rollout show "object did not move" → a high cost → the sample loses the parallel competition, and the mode logic (hysteresis between C3 and repositioning, ≈ lines 1145–1217) switches to a scripted repositioning trajectory that moves the EE to a freshly-sampled contact location and tries again. Contact is established by a non-C3 motion controller; C3 only ever runs from a concrete, in-/near-contact \(\mathbf x_0\), one of several sampled candidates. In the grasp, by contrast, the zero-torque fallback is the command sent to the robot — and it is the cube's only support, so the fallback guarantees the drop. There is no second candidate and no recovery controller.

What to change in the grasp controller, ranked by immediacy
  1. Box the C3 program. Add the torque (INPUT) and finger-velocity (STATE) bounds spelled out in 13.5 via AddLinearConstraint. This should remove the DualInfeasible outright — it is the exact mechanism Push-Anything relies on and is missing in the grasp.
  2. Gate execution on a rollout (13.6). Before applying GetInputSolution, simulate the regularised LCS forward; if the cube falls in the rollout, hold with the PD/q_contact preload instead of the C3 torque.
  3. Swap to C3+ projection (13.8) and keep the regularised contact evaluation (13.7).
  4. Never let zero-torque fallback be the cube's only support (13.4, 13.9). Keep a PD/impedance controller owning "hold the cube," and let C3 only regulate the squeeze magnitude on top of a supported cube — the §12 hybrid split is the structural analogue of Push-Anything keeping the object on a table while C3 only optimises the push.

§15

The C3+ Optimization Problem in Full — Every Variable, Constant, and Term

This section writes out, completely, the optimization problem C3+ solves on every control step of the squeeze phase (§14.5). It names every decision variable, every matrix and constant that feeds it, says which piece belongs to what, gives the exact numbers used in the grasp, and explains what each term is there to do. Dimensions are quoted for the default Stewart–Trinkle model; the Anitescu numbers (§13) are noted where they differ.

15.1  ·  The shape of the problem

It is a finite-horizon Quadratic Program with Complementarity Constraints (QPCC) over a short prediction horizon of \(N\) knots. "Quadratic program" because the cost is quadratic and the dynamics are affine; "with complementarity constraints" because contact forces obey the non-smooth either/or rules of §4. That complementarity is non-convex, so C3+ does not solve the QPCC directly — it splits it with ADMM into a convex QP and a closed-form projection that are reconciled over a fixed number of iterations (§15.5). On each control step the whole thing is re-built (re-linearised) at the current measured state and solved from scratch; only the first input is applied (standard MPC).

15.2  ·  The decision variables (what is being solved for)

For each knot \(k\) the solver carries four blocks, stacked into one per-knot vector \(\mathbf z_k=(\mathbf x_k,\boldsymbol\lambda_k,\mathbf u_k,\boldsymbol\eta_k)\). The \(\boldsymbol\eta_k\) block is what makes it "C3+": ordinary C3 substitutes the gap away, but C3+ keeps it as an explicit variable so the complementarity projection is clean.

Variable Dim (grasp) Index range Meaning
\(\mathbf x_k\), \(k=0..N\) 45 — Full state \((\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q})\): hand joints (16 q), cube pose (7: quat+xyz), hand vel (16), cube vel (6).
\(\mathbf u_k\), \(k=0..N\!-\!1\) 16 — Scaled joint torques \(\tilde{\mathbf u}\); the physical torque is \(\boldsymbol\tau=s_u\tilde{\mathbf u}\).
\(\boldsymbol\lambda_k\), \(k=0..N\!-\!1\) 18 (Anitescu: 12) \([\gamma(3)\,|\,\lambda_n(3)\,|\,\beta(12)]\) Stewart–Trinkle contact impulses: sliding slacks \(\gamma\), normal forces \(\lambda_n\) (entries 3,4,5), friction-pyramid weights \(\beta\).
\(\boldsymbol\eta_k\), \(k=0..N\!-\!1\) 18 (Anitescu: 12) same blocks as \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) The gap vector — complementary partner of \(\boldsymbol\lambda\). Each entry is the "slack" that must be zero wherever its \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) entry is positive.

Per-knot \(\mathbf z_k\in\mathbb R^{97}\) (\(=45+18+16+18\); Anitescu \(85\)). The full decision vector is \(\{\mathbf x_{0:N},\,\boldsymbol\lambda_{0:N-1},\,\mathbf u_{0:N-1},\,\boldsymbol\eta_{0:N-1}\}\).

15.4  ·  The optimization problem, written out

C3+ QPCC — squeeze phase, per control step

\(\displaystyle \min_{\substack{\mathbf x_{0:N},\ \mathbf u_{0:N-1}\\ \boldsymbol\lambda_{0:N-1},\ \boldsymbol\eta_{0:N-1}}}\ \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Bigl[\,(\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x_\star)^\top Q\,(\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x_\star) + \mathbf u_k^\top R\,\mathbf u_k + (\boldsymbol\lambda_k-\boldsymbol\lambda_{\text{des}})^\top W_\lambda(\boldsymbol\lambda_k-\boldsymbol\lambda_{\text{des}})\,\Bigr] + (\mathbf x_N-\mathbf x_\star)^\top Q\,(\mathbf x_N-\mathbf x_\star)\)

\(\text{subject to, for all } k:\)

\(\quad \mathbf x_{k+1} = A\mathbf x_k + B\mathbf u_k + D\boldsymbol\lambda_k + \mathbf d \qquad\textbf{[dynamics]}\)

\(\quad \boldsymbol\eta_k = E\mathbf x_k + F\boldsymbol\lambda_k + H\mathbf u_k + \mathbf c \qquad\textbf{[gap definition]}\)

\(\quad 0 \le \boldsymbol\lambda_k \ \perp\ \boldsymbol\eta_k \ge 0 \qquad\textbf{[complementarity — the non-convex part]}\)

\(\quad \mathbf x_0 = \hat{\mathbf x} \qquad\textbf{[initial state]}\)

\(\quad -\,\tau_{\max}/s_u \ \le\ \mathbf u_k \ \le\ \tau_{\max}/s_u \qquad\textbf{[torque box, per joint]}\)

The cost weights state error, input effort, and force-reference error; the dynamics and gap rows are hard equalities; the complementarity is the only non-convex constraint; the box keeps the feasible set bounded (§13.2). Note the cost has no term on \(\boldsymbol\eta\) directly — \(\boldsymbol\eta\) is pinned entirely by its equality to \(E\mathbf x+F\boldsymbol\lambda+H\mathbf u+\mathbf c\) and by the complementarity.

15.5  ·  How C3+ actually solves it — the ADMM split

The complementarity is dropped from a convex QP and enforced separately by a projection; an iteration reconciles the two. Three quantities per knot: \(\mathbf z\) (the QP copy), \(\boldsymbol\delta\) (the projected copy), \(\mathbf w\) (the scaled dual). Repeated admm_iter = 15 times:

z-update — convex QP (SolveQP, OSQP)

Minimise the cost + dynamics + gap-definition + box, plus the consensus penalty \(\lVert \mathbf z_k-\boldsymbol\delta_k+\mathbf w_k\rVert^2_{G}\). Here \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) and \(\boldsymbol\eta\) are free (complementarity ignored). This is where the torque box and force reference act.

δ-update — projection (SolveSingleProjection)

Project \(\mathbf z+\mathbf w\) onto \(0\le\boldsymbol\lambda\perp\boldsymbol\eta\ge0\) per knot. C3+ does this in closed form: for each contact, whichever of \(\sqrt{w_\lambda}\,\boldsymbol\lambda\) and \(\sqrt{w_\eta}\,\boldsymbol\eta\) is larger wins, the other is set to zero, then both are clipped at \(\ge0\). (Ordinary C3 solves a little QP/MIQP here instead.)

Then the dual update \(\mathbf w \leftarrow (\mathbf w+\mathbf z-\boldsymbol\delta)/\rho\) and \(G \leftarrow \rho\,G\) with \(\rho=\)rho_scale=3, tightening the consensus each iteration. After 15 iterations a final QP produces the trajectory; only \(\mathbf u_0\) (rescaled by \(s_u\)) is applied to the robot. The \(\boldsymbol\eta\) weights \(w_\lambda,w_\eta\) used by the projection come from the diagonal of \(U\); the consensus weight is \(G\).

15.7  ·  What each piece does — the expected behaviour

The load-bearing term — \(w_{\text{cube}}\) in \(Q\)

\(Q\) anchors the cube's world pose and velocity at \(\mathbf x_\star\) with weight 1000. Because gravity sits in \(\mathbf d\), the LCS predicts the cube falling unless contact force resists it, so the only way to keep this big penalty small is to produce a grip. This is where "hold the cube" is enforced — softly, as a cost, not a hard constraint (§ "where holding is enforced"). The grip therefore emerges rather than being commanded.

The supporting terms
  • \(k_{\text{hold}}\) (10) in \(Q\) — keeps the fingers near q_contact (on the faces), so C3 cannot "hold" by wandering to some other configuration.
  • \(R\) (\(s_u^2\cdot0.01\)) — small penalty on torque; regularises \(\mathbf u\), prefers gentle inputs.
  • \(W_\lambda\) (100) + \(\boldsymbol\lambda_{\text{des}}=(1,1,2)\) — seeds a positive normal grip and pins the §8.3 squeeze nullspace, so \(\lambda_n\) comes out near target instead of an arbitrary (or negative) value.
  • torque box (\(\pm100/s_u\)) — bounds the QP's feasible set, eliminating the DualInfeasible unbounded-ray failure (§13.2).
  • complementarity — encodes "push only when touching, slide only when friction saturates"; the projection (§15.5) is what enforces it.
What the whole thing should do — and its known limit

Each step, C3+ predicts the next 0.1 s, finds joint torques whose induced contact forces keep the cube at \(z=0.58\) while respecting the friction cone, and applies the first torque. If the grip the cost asks for satisfies \(\sum_i\mu\lambda_n^i\ge mg\), the cube holds and \(\lambda_n\to(1,1,2)\). The structural caveat from §14 still applies: the "hold" is a soft penalty with no hard floor and no non-C3 fallback, so an under-weighted or lopsided grip shows up as a slow sag rather than a hard failure. Every knob above is a lever on that trade-off; the contact model itself (Stewart–Trinkle vs Anitescu, §13/§ "models you can change") is the deepest one — and for this static hold, Stewart–Trinkle is the correct choice.
