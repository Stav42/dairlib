Three-Finger Grasp: Force Analysis

We grasp the cube with three fingers: index, middle, and thumb. The index and middle fingers each apply a normal force whose magnitude we choose freely. The thumb force is then fully determined by the requirement that the net force and net moment on the cube are both zero. Gravity is ignored for now.

1. Contact Geometry

The cube has side length \(L = 0.06\,\text{m}\) and its center of mass at the origin. We use the cube body frame throughout. All forces are normal to their respective faces — i.e., pointing inward, perpendicular to the surface.

FingerFaceFace normal (inward)Contact pointForce
IndexFace 4 (−Y)\(+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\)\(\mathbf{p}_I = (a,\;{-L/2},\;0)\)\(\mathbf{f}_I = \alpha_I\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\)
MiddleFace 4 (−Y)\(+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\)\(\mathbf{p}_M = (b,\;{-L/2},\;0)\)\(\mathbf{f}_M = \alpha_M\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\)
ThumbFace 3 (\(+Y\))\(-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\)\(\mathbf{p}_T = (c,\;{+L/2},\;0)\)\(\mathbf{f}_T = -\alpha_T\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\)

Here \(a, b\) are the x-offsets of the index and middle finger contacts along face 4, and \(c\) is the x-offset of the thumb contact along face 3. All contacts are at \(z = 0\) (mid-height of the cube). The scalars \(\alpha_I, \alpha_M, \alpha_T > 0\) are the force magnitudes.

Why face 3 for the thumb? Face 4 (−Y) and face 3 (+Y) are directly opposite. The index and middle fingers both push in \(+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\); the only way a single contact force can cancel their combined \(+Y\) component is to push in \(-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\), which is the inward normal of face 3. An adjacent face (e.g. face 1) would produce a force in \(\pm\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), which cannot cancel a \(+Y\) net force.

2. Force Balance

Requiring zero net force:

\[ \mathbf{f}_I + \mathbf{f}_M + \mathbf{f}_T = \mathbf{0} \] \[ \alpha_I\,\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \alpha_M\,\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \alpha_T\,\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{0} \]

This has only a \(Y\)-component, giving one scalar equation:

\[ \boxed{\alpha_T = \alpha_I + \alpha_M} \]

The thumb magnitude is simply the sum of the index and middle magnitudes.

3. Moment Balance

Define moment arms from the cube center: \(\mathbf{r}_k = \mathbf{p}_k\). Since all forces are in \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) and all contacts are at \(z=0\), every cross product \(\mathbf{r}_k \times \mathbf{f}_k\) has only a \(Z\)-component:

\[ \mathbf{r}_k \times \mathbf{f}_k = \begin{pmatrix} x_k \\ y_k \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \times \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ f_k \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ x_k\,f_k \end{pmatrix} \]

So the moment balance reduces to a single scalar equation in \(Z\):

\[ a\,\alpha_I + b\,\alpha_M + c\,(-\alpha_T) = 0 \]

Substituting \(\alpha_T = \alpha_I + \alpha_M\):

\[ a\,\alpha_I + b\,\alpha_M = c\,(\alpha_I + \alpha_M) \] \[ (a - c)\,\alpha_I = (c - b)\,\alpha_M \] \[ \boxed{\frac{\alpha_I}{\alpha_M} = \frac{c - b}{a - c}} \]

This is the key constraint: the ratio of index to middle finger force is set entirely by the x-positions of the three contacts relative to each other.

Special case: symmetric contacts

If the index and middle fingers are placed symmetrically about the face centre (\(a = -\Delta,\; b = +\Delta\)) and the thumb is at the face centre (\(c = 0\)), then:

\[ \frac{\alpha_I}{\alpha_M} = \frac{0 - \Delta}{-\Delta - 0} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha_I = \alpha_M \]

Equal finger forces, and \(\alpha_T = 2\alpha_I\). The thumb must push twice as hard as each finger.

4. Summary

Given contact positions \(a, b, c\), the grasp is fully determined by a single free scalar — choose \(\alpha_I\) freely, then:

\[ \alpha_M = \alpha_I \cdot \frac{a - c}{c - b}, \qquad \alpha_T = \alpha_I + \alpha_M \]

All three forces lie along \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) or \(-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\), so they are automatically in the x-y plane and there are no out-of-plane force or moment components to satisfy.

Note on gravity. When gravity is added, it introduces a \(-Z\) force that cannot be balanced by these purely \(\pm Y\) normal forces. Balancing gravity requires tangential (friction) force components in \(+Z\) at the contact points. This is addressed in a future section.

The manipulation force (to move the cube) lives in the nullspace of the grasp map — forces that produce net body motion without violating the grasp constraints. This is addressed in a separate section.

5. From Forces to Execution

The previous sections established what forces to apply. This section covers how to realise those forces on the physical hand — first by positioning the fingers at the contact points, then by converting desired fingertip forces into joint torques.

5a. Target Contact Points in World Frame

The contact points are defined in the cube body frame and must be transformed to the world frame each timestep using the current cube pose \(X_{WC}\):

\[ \mathbf{p}_k^W = X_{WC} \cdot \mathbf{p}_k^C \]

For the index and middle fingers on face 4 (\(-Y\) face) with x-offsets \(a\) and \(b\), and the thumb on face 3 (\(+Y\) face) with offset \(c\):

\[ \mathbf{p}_I^C = \begin{pmatrix} a \\ -L/2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{p}_M^C = \begin{pmatrix} b \\ -L/2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{p}_T^C = \begin{pmatrix} c \\ +L/2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

These world-frame positions are recomputed every control step as the cube moves. In code this is already handled by GetFaceCentrePositions plus the offset.

5b. Positioning the Fingers (Inverse Kinematics)

To place fingertip \(k\) at \(\mathbf{p}_k^W\), we solve an inverse kinematics (IK) problem over the 16 Allegro joint angles \(\mathbf{q}\):

\[ \text{find } \mathbf{q} \text{ such that } \mathrm{FK}_k(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{p}_k^W \quad \text{for } k \in \{I, M, T\} \]

where \(\mathrm{FK}_k\) is the forward kinematics map for fingertip \(k\). In Drake this is done with drake::multibody::InverseKinematics:

  1. Construct an InverseKinematics object on the plant.
  2. Add a PositionConstraint for each fingertip frame, pinning it to a small ball around \(\mathbf{p}_k^W\).
  3. Call Solve(ik.prog()) to get joint angles \(\mathbf{q}^*\).
  4. Feed \(\mathbf{q}^*\) as the desired configuration to the PD controller (or set them directly as the initial condition before contact).
IK vs. hand-tuned joints. For a fixed cube pose you can hand-tune joint angles once and hard-code them. IK is needed when the cube can be at an arbitrary pose — it recomputes the joint target every time \(X_{WC}\) changes.

5c. Converting Fingertip Forces to Joint Torques

Once in contact, the desired fingertip force \(\mathbf{f}_k \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is converted to joint torques via the geometric Jacobian \(J_k \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times n}\) of the fingertip translational velocity with respect to joint velocities:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau}_k = J_k^\top \mathbf{f}_k \]

The total torque command sent to the hand is the sum over all three contacts:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau}_\text{grasp} = J_I^\top \mathbf{f}_I + J_M^\top \mathbf{f}_M + J_T^\top \mathbf{f}_T \]

In Drake, \(J_k\) is computed with plant.CalcJacobianTranslationalVelocity(context, JacobianWrtVariable::kV, fingertip_frame, p_tip, world_frame, world_frame, &J).

Why \(J^\top\)? The principle of virtual work states that the joint torques required to produce a given endpoint force equal \(J^\top f\). This is the transpose (not the inverse) of the Jacobian.

5d. Control Loop

Each timestep the following steps execute in order:

  1. Read current cube pose \(X_{WC}\) via EvalBodyPoseInWorld.
  2. Compute world-frame contact points \(\mathbf{p}_k^W = X_{WC}\,\mathbf{p}_k^C\).
  3. Compute force magnitudes from GetGraspMagnitudes; express each force in world frame (e.g. \(\mathbf{f}_I = \alpha_I\,R_{WC}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\)).
  4. Compute fingertip Jacobians \(J_I, J_M, J_T\) at the current joint state.
  5. Compute grasp torques \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_\text{grasp} = \sum_k J_k^\top \mathbf{f}_k\).
  6. Add \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_\text{grasp}\) to the PD torques (or replace PD once in contact).

Steps 3–6 can be implemented as a new Drake LeafSystem that takes the plant state as input and outputs joint torques, sitting alongside the existing PdJointController.
