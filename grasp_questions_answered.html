Question 1   CORRECT & SHARP

“Is the contact pair made between any part of the finger tip and the cube, or between any part of any link of the finger and the cube?”

This is exactly the right question, and the code gives a crisp answer: neither the whole finger nor a magic point — it is one specific geometry that you named, the fingertip collision body, versus the cube's one collision body.

Look at how the pairs are built (lines 297–310):

lcs_index_geom  = collision geom of body "link_3_tip"
lcs_middle_geom = collision geom of body "link_7_tip"
lcs_thumb_geom  = collision geom of body "link_15_tip"
lcs_cube_geom   = collision geom of the cube (the FIRST one, index [0])

contact_pairs = {
   (link_3_tip  , cube),
   (link_7_tip  , cube),
   (link_15_tip , cube)
}

So the answer breaks into two halves:

Which link?Only the named tip link
Which link counts as "the finger" in the model? ONLY link_3_tip, link_7_tip, link_15_tip. Middle phalanges (link_1, link_2, …) are not in contact_pairs, so the LCS/C3 is blind to them. Not "any link".
Which point on that tip? Not a hard-coded point. Drake computes the closest pair of points between the two full geometries and measures the gap there. So it is "any part of the tip geometry" — whichever part is nearest the cube surface at that instant.

Formally, each pair \(i\) contributes one signed distance function \(\phi_i(q)\), the gap between geometry \(A_i\) (the tip) and geometry \(B\) (the cube):

\[ \phi_i(q) \;=\; \min_{\mathbf{a}\in A_i,\ \mathbf{b}\in B}\ \| \mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\| \quad\text{(negative if they interpenetrate).} \]

This \(\phi_i\) is the first row of your \(\eta_k\) block, and it is the thing that is complementary to the normal force: \(0 \le \phi_i \perp \lambda_n^i \ge 0\). In words: if there is a gap (\(\phi_i>0\)) the normal force must be zero; a force can only appear when the gap closes (\(\phi_i=0\)).

The catch you should remember. The cube is a single collision box, so "index touches face 4" vs "index touches face 3" is not encoded anywhere — the pair is just tip ↔ whole cube. Which face gets pressed is decided by where you put the tip, not by the contact definition. (Your write-up's "face 1 / face 2" labels are your bookkeeping, not the solver's.)

Question 2   CORRECT & CRUCIAL

“How can we ensure that no other thing touches the cube, so the interaction is only through these three defined points?”

This is the single most important question in the whole file, and you are right to worry. Here is why it matters, stated as bluntly as possible: there are two different worlds, and they disagree.

The LCS / C3 "model" worldThe SAP "physics" world (the real sim)
Who computes contact? LCSFactory::LinearizePlantToLCS on the lcs_plant sim_plant with SAP, inside simulator.AdvanceTo
Which contacts exist? Only the 3 you listed. Nothing else can produce a \(\lambda\). Every unfiltered geometry pair in the scene. Knuckle, palm, ring finger — all real.
Effect of a stray touch Invisible. C3 never sees the force. Produces a real force that shoves the cube.

So if a non-modelled body (say link_2, a middle phalanx, or the palm) bumps the cube, the physics applies a force \(\mathbf{f}_\text{stray}\) that C3's plan never accounted for. The cube's true update is

\[ x_{k+1} \;=\; \underbrace{A x_k + B u_k + C\lambda_k + D}_{\text{what C3 predicts}} \;+\; \underbrace{C_\text{stray}\,\lambda_\text{stray}}_{\text{surprise, not in the model}}, \]

and now the plan is wrong. This is called model mismatch, and it is the classic way these grasp controllers silently fail.

How you actually guarantee it — three levers

  1. Collision filtering (the real tool). Drake lets you switch off collisions between chosen geometry sets with CollisionFilterManager. The code already does this once (lines 220–235) — it excludes the thumb links from hitting the palm: 
    collision_filter_manager().Apply(
   ExcludeBetween( {thumb geoms link_12..link_15_tip},
                   {palm_link geoms} ));
    To make your guarantee real, you would add analogous filters so that every non-tip finger geometry is excluded from the cube, leaving only link_3_tip, link_7_tip, link_15_tip able to collide with it. That makes the physics world match the model world by construction.
  2. Geometry / pose design. Start the fingers folded so only the three tips can reach the cube. The code does this: it starts at q_pregrasp (1 cm outside the faces) and only the tips are aimed at the faces. Good practice, but it is a hope, not a guarantee — a bad trajectory could still swing a knuckle in.
  3. Detection / bail-out. The loop reads get_contact_results_output_port every step (lines 483–497) and checks each contact's two body indices against the tip/cube pair. Today it uses this only to decide "is the tip touching?"; you could extend it to detect a non-tip body touching the cube and flag the mismatch.
Honest status of the current code. It does not yet fully enforce your Q2. The only filter present is thumb-vs-palm (to stop a numerical blow-up), not other-links-vs-cube. So right now the guarantee rests on lever 2 (careful geometry) alone. If you want the guarantee to be airtight, add lever 1.

Question 3   HALF RIGHT — good instinct, wrong mechanism

“If we set the fingertip close to the cube face, will the optimization find a local-minimum trajectory that touches the near face, rather than an erroneous one that takes a long way off?”

Your worry about local minima and "taking a long way off" is a legitimate worry for trajectory optimizers in general. But it does not describe what this file does, because of one design fact you may have missed:

In this code, C3 does not do the reaching. The touching is scripted.

Read the phase machine (lines 465–722). There are two phases:

PhaseWhat drives the fingersRole of C3
kReach A PD controller + cubic spline from q_pregrasp → q_contact (lines 513–530). Pure scripted motion. Not running at all.
Handoff Latches when the sim reports all three tips actually touching (lines 505–511, 533). The LCS is linearized right at the contact configuration (line 544).
kC3 C3/ADMM torques, once the cube pin is released. Maintains the grasp; it never had to find it.

So two corrections to the premise:

Correction A — "will the optimization find a trajectory to touch?" — No, it doesn't have to.

Contact is established by the PD reach, and confirmed by the real physics before C3 ever starts. C3 is handed a state that is already touching. There is no "long way off" search for C3 to get wrong, because C3 is only switched on after contact.

Correction B — C3 is a local, near-contact MPC, not a global planner.

Your dynamics matrices \((A,B,C,D)\) are a linearization, taken at the current state \(x^\star\) each step (the relinearize path, lines 685–693):

\[ x_{k+1} \approx A x_k + B u_k + C \lambda_k + D,\qquad A=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{x^\star},\ \dots \]

A linearization is only trustworthy near \(x^\star\). C3 is therefore physically incapable of planning a big swing from far away — it optimizes a small QP around the current contact configuration over a short horizon (\(N=5\) knots, \(dt=0.04\)s ≈ 0.2s look-ahead). "Taking a long way off" is not a failure mode it can even reach into.

So why does putting the tip close still matter? (Your instinct was right — for a different reason.)

Proximity matters for two concrete, code-level reasons — just not the one you named:

  1. It keeps the complementarity "active." C3 can only generate a squeeze force \(\lambda_n^i\) when the gap is closed, because of \(0 \le \phi_i \perp \lambda_n^i \ge 0\). If \(\phi_i > 0\) (tip away from the face) the model forces \(\lambda_n^i = 0\) — no grip is even representable. That is why the code seats the tips 5 mm inside the faces at q_contact (cube_size - 0.01, line 353) and PD-holds them there — so \(\phi_i \le 0\) and a real force can exist. The handoff even prints the gaps and expects them \(\le 0\) (lines 549–555).
  2. It keeps the linearization valid. Being close means the small motions C3 commands stay in the region where \((A,B,C,D)\) are accurate, so its predictions don't drift from the SAP physics.
The subtle part you were reaching for. Even with tips in contact, "grip harder" is a whole family of solutions (a nullspace): index/middle/thumb forces can all scale up together while still balancing (this is the ratio result \(\alpha_I/\alpha_M=(c-b)/(a-c)\) and \(\alpha_T=\alpha_I+\alpha_M\) from grasp_theory_critique.html). A cost minimizer left alone would pick the cheapest member — zero squeeze — and drop the cube. The code fixes this two ways: (i) the cube anchor w_cube makes "cube falls" expensive, so C3 must supply \(\sum_i \mu\lambda_n^i \ge mg\); and (ii) the force reference w_lambda with target alpha_m (thumb = \(2\alpha_m\)) picks a specific, non-zero grip out of the nullspace. So the trajectory C3 "finds" is pinned by the costs, not left to chance. That is the real version of your local-minimum worry — and it is handled.

One-paragraph summary

Q1 and Q2 are the correct, important questions, and the code confirms your framing: a contact pair is one named tip geometry against the one cube geometry (Q1), and the model only knows those three pairs while the physics knows all of them, so you must enforce isolation with collision filtering / geometry / detection (Q2 — currently only partially enforced). Q3 has the right spirit but the wrong picture: in this file the touching is done by a scripted PD reach and confirmed by real physics before C3 starts, and C3 is a short-horizon local MPC linearized at contact — so it never searches for a way to touch and cannot "take a long way off." Proximity still matters, but to keep the complementarity active (\(\phi_i \le 0\) so a force is even allowed) and the linearization valid — and the risk you were sensing (many valid grips, cheapest = no grip) is real and is handled by the cube anchor plus the force reference.

Part 2 — Making C3 Actually Hold the Cube

This part is the log of a long debugging session: we tried to get the three fingers to hold the cube in place using C3, watched it fail in several different ways, and eventually got it to hold — but not the way we first expected. Bottom line up front:

  • FAILED Applying C3's torque directly → the hand goes limp, cube drops.
  • WORKS A two-layer design: C3 plans, a low-level OSC executes.
  • CAVEAT The hold is done by an impedance controller; C3 only supplies the grip force. Making C3's own plan hold the cube is still open.

Every claim below is checked against the actual code and the printed diagnostics.

6. The setup, in one picture

Phase 1 (reach): a scripted PD controller drives the three fingertips onto the cube faces while the cube is pinned in place. Phase 2 (hold): the pin is released and C3 is supposed to keep the cube where it is. State vector (\(n_x = 45\)):

\[ x = \big(\underbrace{q_\text{hand}}_{16},\ \underbrace{q^o}_{\text{cube quat }4},\ \underbrace{p^o}_{\text{cube xyz }3},\ \underbrace{\dot q_\text{hand}}_{16},\ \underbrace{\omega^o,\dot p^o}_{\text{cube vel }6}\big). \]
First fix (mundane but real): the reach needed kp≈200. At the default kp=10 the PD was too soft to seat the fingers against gravity — only the thumb ever made contact, so the handoff to C3 never fired. Lesson: rule out the boring failures (a too-weak reach) before blaming the concept.

7. Why applying C3's torque directly fails the core bug

The obvious thing is to take C3's optimal input \(u_0\) and send it to the motors: tau = s_u · GetInputSolution()[0]. When we did this, the instant the pin released the commanded torque collapsed from ~45 Nm to ~0.2 Nm — the hand went limp and the cube free-fell. No amount of tuning (horizon \(N\), ADMM iterations, cube weight) changed this. It is structural.

7a. The "free \(\lambda\)" degeneracy

Recall the contact model. The LCS dynamics and complementarity are

\[ x_{k+1} = A x_k + B u_k + C\lambda_k + D,\qquad 0 \le \eta_k \perp \lambda_k \ge 0,\quad \eta_k = E x_k + F u_k + H \lambda_k + c . \]

C3 solves this with ADMM, which splits the hard problem into two sub-problems that each keep their own copy of the variables (more on this in §10). The important consequence: in the cost-minimizing sub-problem, the contact force \(\lambda\) is a free decision variable. So when C3 asks "how do I keep the cube from falling?", the cheapest answer is to imagine a contact force \(\lambda\) that holds it — without commanding any torque \(u\) to actually produce that force.

The optimizer therefore returns \(u \approx 0\) with a large "paper" \(\lambda\). Physically, zero torque means the fingers relax, the real contact force vanishes, and the cube drops. C3 reports a grip it never generates.

Why the cube-pose cost doesn't save it. We do anchor the cube with a big Q-cost (\(w_\text{cube}=1000\)). But the linearization is taken at the penetrating, supported configuration, where the existing penetration already supplies \(C\lambda\) that balances gravity in \(D\). So in the model, "do nothing" (\(u=0\)) keeps the cube at the reference at zero cost. The intended logic — "gravity drift forces C3 to find grip" — breaks because in the linear model the cube doesn't fall at \(u=0\).

7b. The complementarity gate is an anti-stabilizer

There's a second, related trap. A finger can only push when the gap is closed: \(0 \le \phi \perp \lambda_n \ge 0\). In simulation the true gap hovers at \(\phi \approx 0 \pm 1\,\text{mm}\) (stiff-contact equilibrium noise). The moment the model reads \(\phi > 0\), the projected \(\lambda_n\) is forced to \(0\). A good grip law needs the opposite sign,

\[ \frac{\partial(\text{press})}{\partial \phi} < 0 \quad\text{(gap opens} \Rightarrow \text{push harder)}, \]

but complementarity gives "gap opens \(\Rightarrow\) stop pushing." So \((\phi>0,\ \lambda=0)\) is an absorbing state: no press \(\Rightarrow\) gap stays open \(\Rightarrow\) no press. Combined with the abrupt 45 → 0.2 Nm handoff (which flicks the cube loose), the cube's fate is sealed within one control interval — two different runs produced bit-for-bit identical post-release trajectories, proving the failure happens before anything downstream can react.

8. The right architecture: planner + executor

Reading the real push-anything pipeline (franka_sim_anything.pmd) settled it. That system is not one program applying C3's torque. It is two processes talking over LCM: 

C3 controller (slow)  ──►  publishes TARGETS  ──►  OSC controller (fast)  ──►  torques  ──►  robot
                           • end-effector position
                           • end-effector force  (C3's planned contact force)

C3 never sends torque to the robot. It publishes targets — including a contact-force target — and a low-level Operational-Space Controller (OSC) turns those into joint torques, at high rate, respecting friction cones and torque limits. The OSC's own plant is robot-only; the object lives only inside C3. Our monolithic loop was missing this entire executor layer. Everything we had been hand-rolling (\(J^\top\lambda\), PD hacks) was a crude stand-in for the OSC.

9. The OSC we built (an inverse-dynamics QP)

We wrote a small OSC over the 16-DOF hand, solved every control step with MathematicalProgram + OSQP. It is a "give me your goals, I'll find the torques" solver. Decision variables: joint accelerations \(\dot v\), joint torques \(\tau\), and fingertip contact forces \(f_i\) (force the robot exerts on the cube). It minimizes weighted tracking of fingertip positions and contact forces:

\[ \min_{\dot v,\ \tau,\ f}\ \sum_i w_p\big\|J_i \dot v + \dot J_i v - a_i^\text{des}\big\|^2 + \sum_i w_f\big\|f_i - f_i^\text{des}\big\|^2 + \text{(reg)} \]

subject to the hand equations of motion, a friction pyramid, and the torque box:

\[ M_h \dot v + C_h - \tau_{g,h} = \tau - \textstyle\sum_i J_i^\top f_i,\qquad \hat n_i^\top f_i \ge 0,\ \ |\,\hat t_j^\top f_i| \le \tfrac{\mu}{\sqrt2}\,\hat n_i^\top f_i,\qquad |\tau| \le \tau_\max . \]

The desired fingertip acceleration is a task-space PD, \(a_i^\text{des} = K_p(p_i^\text{des}-p_i) - K_d\,\dot p_i\), and the force target \(f_i^\text{des} = f_{n,i}\,\hat n_i\) with \(f_{n,i} = \max\!\big(\lambda_{n,i}^{\text{(C3, projected)}},\ \alpha_i\big)\) — C3's grip, floored so it never drops to zero across the \(\phi\approx0\) hover band (the --force_floor idea).

This held the cube. With the grip floor on and the fingertip positions targeted at the grasp points of the upright reference cube, the cube stayed up (small droop / tilt, fixed by raising \(K_p\)). First stable hold of the whole session.

10. Is C3's plan any good? A diagnostic framework

A working hold isn't the same as "C3 is doing the work." To judge the plan itself we used a three-way test. A controller fails in exactly one of these ways:

FactorMeaningHow we measure it
1 Plan has high cost — C3 knows it can't win. Plot the planned cube-\(z\) over the horizon + per-knot cost. Does the plan keep the cube up?
2a Low cost, but plan is infeasible (complementarity error). ADMM consensus residual \(\|z_k-\delta_k\|\) (below).
2b Plan is realistic, but the low-level controller can't track it. Compare the plan's predicted next state \(x_1\) to the state actually achieved one step later.

10a. The two ADMM copies (what \(\|z-\delta\|\) means)

Complementarity is combinatorial, so ADMM splits the problem and keeps two copies of the solution:

  • \(z\) (GetFullSolution) — the QP copy: obeys the linear dynamics + cost, but ignores complementarity, so its \(\lambda\) can go negative (unphysical).
  • \(\delta\) (GetDualDeltaSolution) — the projected copy: forced to satisfy \(0\le\eta\perp\lambda\ge0\), so it's physically feasible.

ADMM haggles the two toward each other; at convergence \(z=\delta\) and you have one trajectory that is both cost-optimal and feasible. So \(\|z-\delta\|\) is literally "how far apart are the cheat-physics plan and the obey-physics plan." Large \(\Rightarrow\) no single trajectory is both, i.e. the committed plan is infeasible. (This is why the raw \(\lambda\) we printed swung negative while the projected \(\lambda\) stayed small and positive.)

10b. What the numbers said

FactorReading (after pin release)Verdict
1 With the grip floor on, planned cube \(z\) stays near target and cost is moderate. Mostly NO — C3 thinks it holds.
2a \(\|z-\delta\|\) drops from ~30–90 (pinned) to ~1–2.5 (released) — non-zero but small. Minor (3 ADMM iters don't fully converge).
2b Cube tracked to ~1 cm, but hand-joint error 0.5–0.8 rad. YES — we weren't tracking C3's plan at all.

11. The punchline: it's the plan, not the tracker honest result

Factor 2b was large because the OSC's position target was a hand-designed geometric reference (the fixed grasp points), not C3's plan. So we made the OSC actually track C3's planned next state \(x_1\) (\(q_1, v_1\)). Result: the cube fell. And the diagnostics showed why — C3's planned cube-\(z\) itself drifts down over the horizon, with cost \(10^5\)–\(10^6\). C3 plans \(u\approx 0\); its plan contains no restoring action. Tracking a passive plan faithfully reproduces its passivity.

So the two experiments together are the answer: when we impose an external restoring reference (ignore C3's plan) → the cube holds. When we faithfully execute C3's plan → the cube falls. Therefore the bottleneck is C3's plan (a Factor-1 passivity), not the low-level controller. The root is the free-\(\lambda\) degeneracy of §7a: C3 fakes the grip in its math and plans zero effort.

11a. Reference vs. plan — the key distinction

The geometric target works because it encodes C3's goal (cube at 0.58, upright), which is a fixed spring pulling the cube back. C3's plan is its (currently passive) prediction, which just follows the cube wherever it sags. Targeting the goal restores; targeting the plan does not.

12. Where it stands — the switch

The current binary is a working hybrid with a config switch for the two position-target sources:

FlagOSC fingertip targetBehavior
--fk_target=false (default) fixed grasp points on the upright reference cube HOLDS impedance regulation holds; C3 supplies grip force
--fk_target=true forward-kinematics of C3's planned hand config \(q_1\) SAGS honest test of executing C3's passive plan

Other knobs that matter: --force_floor (the commanded grip — this is the friction-threshold study; sweep it down until the cube slips), --osc_kp (how stiffly the hand regulates cube pose), and the plan-quality diagnostics --plan_debug (the F1/F2a/F2b lines).

12a. The open problem

To make C3 itself hold (and eventually move) the cube, the fix is upstream: kill the free-\(\lambda\) passivity so the plan contains real corrective effort. Candidates, in order of promise: many more ADMM iterations / a stronger consensus weight so \(z\) and \(\delta\) agree (drives \(\|z-\delta\|\to0\)), or a reformulation that ties \(\lambda\) to the input. Only once the plan is non-passive does "track C3's plan" (--fk_target=true) become the correct executor — and the same machinery then drives cube manipulation (e.g. the up/down bob), where tracking C3's planned cube trajectory is exactly right and the fixed reference would fight it.

13. One-paragraph summary of Part 2

We could not hold the cube by sending C3's torque directly, because C3's optimizer treats the grip force as a free variable and so plans near-zero effort (the "free-\(\lambda\)" degeneracy), compounded by the complementarity gate that zeroes the grip whenever the contact gap flickers positive. The correct fix is the push-anything architecture: C3 plans, a fast OSC (an inverse-dynamics QP tracking fingertip position + C3's contact force under friction and torque limits) executes. That holds the cube — but the holding is done by an impedance reference to the cube's goal pose, with C3 contributing only the grip magnitude; when we instead track C3's own plan the cube falls, proving the remaining bottleneck is the plan's passivity, not the controller. A --fk_target flag switches between the two, and the open work is making C3's plan non-passive so it can hold — and then move — the cube on its own.
