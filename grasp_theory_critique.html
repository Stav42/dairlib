§0

Foundations — Forces, Wrenches, and the Two Jacobians

We build everything from scratch. Start with the simplest object.

Definition — Force

Force  \(\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^3\)

A force is a 3D vector. It has a direction and a magnitude. Applying force \(\mathbf{f}\) to the centre of mass of a rigid body causes it to accelerate linearly: \(m\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{f}\). Nothing more.

Definition — Torque

Torque  \(\boldsymbol{\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^3\)

A torque is a 3D vector that causes a rigid body to angularly accelerate: \(I\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \boldsymbol{\tau}\). A torque about the \(z\)-axis spins the body around \(z\). Torque and force are different physical quantities — torque has units N·m, force has units N.

Definition — Wrench

Wrench  \(\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^6\)

A wrench stacks a force and a torque into one 6D vector:

\[ \mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{f} \\ \boldsymbol{\tau} \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6 \]

A wrench fully describes the loading on a rigid body: the first 3 entries drive linear acceleration, the last 3 drive angular acceleration. A rigid body has 6 degrees of freedom (3 translation, 3 rotation), so a wrench has exactly 6 components — one per DOF.

How a single finger produces a wrench on the cube

Here is the key point. Suppose finger \(i\) presses on the cube surface with force \(\mathbf{f}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3\) at contact point \(\mathbf{p}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3\), where \(\mathbf{p}_i\) is measured from the cube's centre of mass.

Because the force is applied away from the centre, it does two things at once:

  • It pushes the cube — contributing \(\mathbf{f}_i\) to the net force.
  • It rotates the cube — the lever arm \(\mathbf{p}_i\) creates a torque \(\mathbf{p}_i \times \mathbf{f}_i\).

The cross product \(\mathbf{p}_i \times \mathbf{f}_i\) encodes exactly this lever-arm effect. Concretely, if \(\mathbf{p}_i = (p_x, p_y, p_z)\) and \(\mathbf{f}_i = (f_x, f_y, f_z)\):

\[ \mathbf{p}_i \times \mathbf{f}_i = \begin{pmatrix} p_y f_z - p_z f_y \\ p_z f_x - p_x f_z \\ p_x f_y - p_y f_x \end{pmatrix} \]

So the full wrench that finger \(i\) exerts on the cube is:

\[ \mathbf{w}_i = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{f}_i \\ \mathbf{p}_i \times \mathbf{f}_i \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6 \]
Definition — Net Wrench

Net Wrench from \(n\) contacts

With \(n\) fingers each pressing at their own point, the net wrench on the cube is the sum of all individual wrenches — just Newton's third law applied to all contacts at once:

\[ \mathbf{w}_\text{net} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{w}_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{f}_i \\ \mathbf{p}_i \times \mathbf{f}_i \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6 \]

This is what Newton's laws act on. If \(\mathbf{w}_\text{net} = \mathbf{0}\), the cube is in static equilibrium — not accelerating, not rotating.

The Grasp Matrix \(G\) — packaging the geometry

The net wrench is linear in each \(\mathbf{f}_i\), which means we can write it as a single matrix multiplication. Stack all contact forces into one long vector:

\[ \mathbf{f}_c = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{f}_1 \\ \mathbf{f}_2 \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{f}_n \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3n} \]

For each contact \(i\), define the \(6 \times 3\) block:

\[ G_i = \begin{pmatrix} I_3 \\ [\mathbf{p}_i]_\times \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 3} \]

where \(I_3\) is the \(3\times 3\) identity (passes the force through unchanged), and \([\mathbf{p}_i]_\times\) is the skew-symmetric matrix that computes the cross product — i.e., \([\mathbf{p}_i]_\times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{p}_i \times \mathbf{v}\) for any vector \(\mathbf{v}\):

\[ [\mathbf{p}]_\times = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -p_z & p_y \\ p_z & 0 & -p_x \\ -p_y & p_x & 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

Assemble all blocks side by side to get the Grasp Matrix:

\[ G = \begin{bmatrix} G_1 & G_2 & \cdots & G_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 3n} \]

Then the net wrench is simply:

\[ \mathbf{w} = G\,\mathbf{f}_c \]
What G encodes

\(G\) contains only geometry: where each contact point \(\mathbf{p}_i\) sits on the cube. It says nothing about the robot. It maps from contact force space (\(\mathbb{R}^{3n}\)) to object wrench space (\(\mathbb{R}^6\)). It changes whenever the cube moves or rotates, because \(\mathbf{p}_i\) is defined in the world frame and tracks with the cube pose.

The Robot Jacobian \(J\) — a completely different map

The robot has joint angles \(\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_j}\). Moving the joints at rate \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}\) moves the fingertips at rate \(\dot{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3n}\):

\[ \dot{\mathbf{x}} = J\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}, \qquad J \in \mathbb{R}^{3n \times n_j} \]

Transposing this (via the principle of virtual work), contact forces \(\mathbf{f}_c\) at the fingertips map to joint torques \(\boldsymbol{\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_j}\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \mathbf{f}_c, \qquad J^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n_j \times 3n} \]
What J encodes

\(J\) contains only robot kinematics: how joint motion propagates to fingertip motion. It says nothing about the cube. It maps from contact force space (\(\mathbb{R}^{3n}\)) to joint torque space (\(\mathbb{R}^{n_j}\)). It changes whenever the robot's joint angles change.

The same contact force vector \(\mathbf{f}_c\) feeds both maps simultaneously. Through \(G\) it determines what the cube feels — how it accelerates. Through \(J^\top\) it determines what the robot does — the torques required at each joint. Controlling \(\mathbf{f}_c\) lets you control both at once, which is the whole point.

§1

The Proposed Idea

Summary

Apply an equal inward force at the centre of each of the 4 lateral faces of a cube. Convert those forces to joint torques via the robot Jacobian \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \mathbf{f}\). Then apply additional forces in the nullspace of that Jacobian so that reorientation tasks do not disturb the grasp.

§2

What Is Correct

Valid

Jacobian transpose maps fingertip forces to joint torques

The relationship \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \mathbf{f}_c\) is the standard, correct way to convert contact forces in task space to joint torques via the principle of virtual work. This direction of the derivation is sound.

Valid

Decomposing contact forces into grasp and task components

The idea of splitting contact forces into a component that maintains the grasp without moving the object, and a component that produces a desired wrench on the object, is well-founded in grasp theory. The two components can coexist without interfering — in object space — under the right conditions.

§3

The Critiques

1

Wrong Jacobian — two maps are being conflated

There are two distinct linear maps at play, and they must not be confused:

MapNotationWhat it does
Grasp matrix \(G\) \(\mathbf{w} = G\,\mathbf{f}_c\) Maps contact forces to net wrench on the object
Robot Jacobian \(J\) \(\dot{\mathbf{x}} = J\dot{\mathbf{q}},\quad \boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \mathbf{f}\) Maps joint velocities to fingertip velocities (and forces back)

The nullspace of \(G\) contains contact forces that produce zero net wrench on the object — these are the internal squeeze forces that hold the cube without accelerating it. This is the correct space for grasp forces.

The nullspace of \(J\) contains joint motions that produce zero fingertip motion — robot self-motions from kinematic redundancy. An entirely different concept. Applying forces "in the nullspace of \(J\)" does not decouple the grasp from the task.

The correct statement: decompose contact forces into \(\ker(G)\) (internal) and task components. Task forces produce the desired cube wrench; internal forces squeeze without disturbing the task.

2

Four lateral face contacts do not achieve full 3D wrench closure

For complete force closure in 3D, the hand must resist arbitrary forces and torques in all 6 degrees of freedom. Four contacts on the lateral faces all lie in the same horizontal plane:

DOFResisted?Reason
Lateral forces \(f_x,\, f_y\) Yes Normal forces point inward horizontally
Torque about vertical axis \(\tau_z\) ConditionallyRequires friction; not guaranteed with equal inward normals alone
Tipping moments \(\tau_x,\, \tau_y\) No All contact points are coplanar — zero moment arm about tilt axes
Vertical force \(f_z\) No Lateral normals have no vertical component; gravity is unresisted

Gravity acts vertically downward. If the 4 contacts are purely lateral, their normal forces have no vertical component. The cube will fall unless friction is large enough — and even then, friction forces must be explicitly planned for, not assumed.

3

Equal forces at face centres is overly restrictive

Symmetric equal forces are a special case, valid only for a perfectly symmetric cube in a perfectly symmetric hand. In practice:

  • Finger placements will not be exactly at face centres.
  • Asymmetric tasks (rotating about one axis) require unequal contact forces.
  • The optimiser for task forces needs freedom to vary individual contact magnitudes to stay within friction cones.

Locking to equal forces removes that freedom and will cause infeasibility in the friction cone constraints for non-trivial tasks.

4

The grasp matrix changes as the cube rotates

When the cube is free-floating, both the robot Jacobian \(J\) and the grasp matrix \(G\) depend on the current configuration of the robot and the current pose of the cube. As the cube rotates — the stated goal — the contact geometry changes, \(G\) changes, and \(\ker(G)\) changes. This is not a static decomposition. It must be recomputed at every control timestep.

5

No friction cone constraints

The formulation says nothing about friction. Every contact force must lie within its friction cone:

\[ f_n \geq 0, \qquad \|\mathbf{f}_t\| \leq \mu\, f_n \]

The first condition says contacts can only push, never pull. The second bounds the tangential (sliding) force by the normal force times the friction coefficient. If task forces push any contact force outside its cone, the finger slips. These constraints are not optional — they are the hard feasibility condition that makes a grasp physically realizable.

§4

The Corrected Formulation

The right framework is grasp force decomposition. Total contact forces split into an internal component (living in the nullspace of \(G\)) and a task component (which produces the desired wrench on the cube):

\[ \mathbf{f}_c \;=\; \mathbf{f}_\text{internal} + \mathbf{f}_\text{task} \]

Subject to:

\(G\,\mathbf{f}_\text{internal} = \mathbf{0}\)    — internal forces; zero net wrench on the cube (the squeeze)

\(G\,\mathbf{f}_\text{task} = \mathbf{w}_\text{desired}\)    — task forces; produce the desired cube wrench (e.g. a rotation)

\(\boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \mathbf{f}_c\)    — convert total contact forces to joint torques

\(\mathbf{f}_c \in \mathcal{FC}\)    — hard constraint: all contacts must stay inside their friction cones

\(G,\; J \;\text{updated each timestep}\)    — both depend on robot config + cube pose

\(\mathbf{f}_\text{internal}\) lives in \(\ker(G)\) — that is the squeeze force. \(\mathbf{f}_\text{task}\) is determined by what wrench you want to apply to rotate the cube. The two do not fight each other in object space, but both contribute to joint torques and both must independently satisfy the friction cone constraints.

Important nuance

This is a QP at each timestep

Finding \(\mathbf{f}_c\) is itself an optimisation problem. A common approach is a quadratic program (QP) that minimises joint torque or fingertip force magnitude, subject to the desired object wrench and friction cone constraints. This is precisely what controllers like OSC and C3 do under the hood.

§5

Force Closure & Friction

Force closure is the property that determines whether a grasp can resist any external disturbance. Understanding it requires looking carefully at what kind of contact is assumed.

Definition

Force Closure

A grasp achieves force closure if, for any external wrench \(\mathbf{w}_\text{ext} \in \mathbb{R}^6\) applied to the object, there exist contact forces \(\mathbf{f}_c\) — each inside its friction cone — such that the grasp matrix equation is satisfied:

\[ G\,\mathbf{f}_c = -\mathbf{w}_\text{ext} \]

Equivalently: the set of achievable wrenches \(\{G\mathbf{f}_c \mid \mathbf{f}_c \in \mathcal{FC}\}\) contains an open ball around the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^6\).

How friction is modelled determines what each contact can contribute.

Frictionless Contact

Force is purely along the inward normal:

\[\mathbf{f}_i = f_n\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}_i, \quad f_n \geq 0\]

The contact can only push. No tangential resistance. Each contact contributes a single ray to the wrench space.

Frictional Contact

Force has a normal component and a bounded tangential component:

\[\mathbf{f}_i = f_n\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}_i + \mathbf{f}_t\] \[\|\mathbf{f}_t\| \leq \mu\, f_n\]

The contact contributes a cone of wrench directions. Much richer wrench space with fewer contacts.

Place the cube at the origin with side length \(L\). The four lateral contact points and their inward normals are:

\[ \mathbf{p}_1 = \!\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{L}{2}\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\!, \hat{\mathbf{n}}_1 = \!\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{p}_2 = \!\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac{L}{2}\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\!, \hat{\mathbf{n}}_2 = \!\begin{pmatrix}+1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} \] \[ \mathbf{p}_3 = \!\begin{pmatrix}0\\\tfrac{L}{2}\\0\end{pmatrix}\!, \hat{\mathbf{n}}_3 = \!\begin{pmatrix}0\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{p}_4 = \!\begin{pmatrix}0\\-\tfrac{L}{2}\\0\end{pmatrix}\!, \hat{\mathbf{n}}_4 = \!\begin{pmatrix}0\\+1\\0\end{pmatrix} \]

The wrench contributed by contact \(i\) is the 6-vector stacking the linear force and the resulting moment about the object's centre:

\[ \mathbf{w}_i = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{f}_i \\ \mathbf{p}_i \times \mathbf{f}_i\end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6 \]

Case 1 — No friction

Each contact force is purely normal: \(\mathbf{f}_i = f_{ni}\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}_i\). Because each \(\mathbf{p}_i\) is antiparallel to \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_i\) (both lie along the same coordinate axis), the moment term vanishes:

\[ \mathbf{p}_1 \times \mathbf{f}_1 = \left(\tfrac{L}{2},0,0\right) \times (-f_{n1},0,0) = f_{n1}\underbrace{\left(\tfrac{L}{2},0,0\right) \times (-1,0,0)}_{\text{antiparallel} \Rightarrow\; \mathbf{0}} = \mathbf{0} \]

The same holds for all four contacts. The total achievable wrench is:

\[ \mathbf{w}_\text{total} = \begin{pmatrix}-f_{n1}+f_{n2}\\[2pt]-f_{n3}+f_{n4}\\[2pt]0\\[2pt]0\\[2pt]0\\[2pt]0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad f_{ni} \geq 0 \]
Result — No Force Closure

The achievable wrench space spans only 2 dimensions: \(f_x\) and \(f_y\). Vertical forces (\(f_z\)), and all three moment components (\(\tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z\)), are identically zero regardless of what \(f_{ni}\) values are chosen. The grasp matrix has rank 2, not 6. The cube falls and tips freely.

Case 2 — With friction (\(\mu > 0\))

Now each contact has a tangential component. Take contact 1 at \(\mathbf{p}_1 = (L/2,\,0,\,0)\):

\[ \mathbf{f}_1 = (-f_{n1},\; f_{t1y},\; f_{t1z}), \qquad f_{t1y}^2 + f_{t1z}^2 \leq \mu^2 f_{n1}^2 \]
\[ \mathbf{p}_1 \times \mathbf{f}_1 = \left(\tfrac{L}{2},0,0\right) \times (-f_{n1},\,f_{t1y},\,f_{t1z}) = \begin{pmatrix}0\\-\tfrac{L}{2}f_{t1z}\\+\tfrac{L}{2}f_{t1y}\end{pmatrix} \]

Summing contributions from all four contacts, the six wrench components become:

DOFWhat produces itFrictionlessWith friction
\(f_x\) Normal forces \(f_{n1},\, f_{n2}\) YesYes
\(f_y\) Normal forces \(f_{n3},\, f_{n4}\) YesYes
\(f_z\) \(f_{t1z} + f_{t2z} + f_{t3z} + f_{t4z}\) NoYes
\(\tau_x\) \(\tfrac{L}{2}(f_{t3z} - f_{t4z})\) NoYes
\(\tau_y\) \(\tfrac{L}{2}(-f_{t1z} + f_{t2z})\) NoYes
\(\tau_z\) \(\tfrac{L}{2}(f_{t1y} - f_{t2y} - f_{t3x} + f_{t4x})\) NoYes
Result — Force Closure Achievable

All 6 wrench components can be independently controlled. The grasp matrix has rank 6, and force closure holds — provided \(\mu > 0\) and the normal forces \(f_{ni} > 0\) are actively maintained.

The formal test for force closure

A grasp achieves force closure if and only if the origin lies strictly inside the convex hull of the set of primitive contact wrenches \(\{\mathbf{w}_i \mid \mathbf{f}_i \in \mathcal{FC}_i\}\).

For the frictionless 4-contact case, the achievable wrenches form a 2D flat (only \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) reachable). The origin is on the boundary of a flat — not in the interior of a full-dimensional body. No force closure.

For the frictional case with \(\mu > 0\) and \(f_{ni} > 0\), the friction cones sweep out a full-dimensional body in \(\mathbb{R}^6\), and the origin can be made to lie strictly inside it. Force closure holds.

Practical implications

ScenarioForce closure?Why
4 lateral contacts, no friction No Only \(f_x, f_y\) resisted; \(f_z\) and all moments are zero
4 lateral contacts, \(\mu > 0\), \(f_n > 0\) Yes Friction supplies \(f_z\) and all three moment directions
4 lateral contacts, friction but \(f_n \to 0\) Collapses No normal force → no friction budget → grasp fails
Key insight

The internal forces buy friction capacity

The friction is not automatic — you must actively maintain normal forces \(f_{ni} > 0\) to have a friction budget. This is precisely the role of the internal forces \(\mathbf{f}_\text{internal} \in \ker(G)\): they keep all contacts pressed against the cube without producing any net wrench on it, so there is always a friction reserve available for the task forces to draw on.

This is why the internal/task decomposition is not just mathematically convenient — it is physically necessary. The internal forces are not wasted. They are what makes a frictional grasp robust.

§7

Where C3 and OSC Come Into the Picture

Sections §3–§5 established that computing valid contact forces \(\mathbf{f}_c\) requires solving a constrained optimisation at every timestep. C3 and OSC are the two controllers — operating at different levels — that actually do this on a real robot.

The problem that needs to be solved every timestep

At every control timestep, the robot needs to answer: "given where the cube is right now and where I want it to go, what forces should my fingers apply?" This is a trajectory optimisation problem over a short future horizon \(N\):

\[ \min_{\mathbf{x}_{0:N},\, \mathbf{u}_{0:N-1},\, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0:N-1}} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \ell(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k) + \ell_f(\mathbf{x}_N) \] \[ \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = f(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k) \] \[ 0 \leq \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k \;\perp\; \Phi(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k) \geq 0 \]

Here \(\mathbf{x}_k\) is the full system state (robot joints + object pose), \(\mathbf{u}_k\) is the control input (e.g. end-effector Cartesian force), \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) is the contact force vector, and \(\Phi\) is the signed distance (gap) between contact pairs — positive when separated, zero at contact. The symbol \(\perp\) means complementarity: either the gap is zero (contact is active, force can be nonzero) or the force is zero (no contact). They cannot both be nonzero simultaneously.

This problem is hard to solve in real time because the complementarity constraint makes it non-smooth and combinatorially complex (every contact pair is either on or off — exponentially many combinations).

The contact model: Linear Complementarity System (LCS)

C3 and C3+ (Push Anything) make this tractable by locally linearising the dynamics around the current state to get a Linear Complementarity System (LCS):

\[ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{x}_k + B\mathbf{u}_k + D\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + \mathbf{d} \] \[ 0 \leq \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k \;\perp\; E\mathbf{x}_k + F\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + H\mathbf{u}_k + \mathbf{c} \geq 0 \]

The matrices \(A, B, D, E, F, H\) and vectors \(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d}\) are computed once per timestep by linearising around the current operating point. The contact force vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) is split into:

  • \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^n \in \mathbb{R}^{n_c}\) — normal forces at each of \(n_c\) contact pairs
  • \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^t \in \mathbb{R}^{n_c \cdot n_e}\) — tangential (friction) forces, with \(n_e\) being the number of edges of the polyhedral friction cone approximation

In Push Anything, \(n_e = 4\): the circular Coulomb cone is approximated by a 4-sided pyramid. This turns the nonlinear friction cone constraint into a set of linear inequalities, which is what makes the LCS tractable.

\[ \|\mathbf{f}_t\| \leq \mu f_n \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{approximated by } n_e = 4 \text{ linear faces of a pyramid} \]

C3 and C3+: the high-level planner

C3 — Consensus Complementarity Control (Aydinoglu et al., 2024)

C3 solves the LCS-constrained MPC problem using ADMM (Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers). It splits the problem into two alternating subproblems:

  • Quadratic step: solve a QP over the full trajectory, treating complementarity as soft — this is a smooth, fast solve.
  • Projection step: project the contact forces onto the feasible complementarity set \(\{(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, \boldsymbol{\eta}) : 0 \leq \boldsymbol{\lambda} \perp \boldsymbol{\eta} \geq 0\}\) — this is done independently per timestep and can be parallelised.

The output of C3 is a desired trajectory: a sequence of desired end-effector positions or forces over the horizon. C3 runs at the high level — it does not directly produce joint torques.

C3+ — Consensus Complementarity Control Plus (Push Anything, 2026)

C3+ improves on C3 with a faster projection step (Fig. 5 in the paper: a linear switching boundary replaces the more expensive original projection), enabling real-time performance even with up to 19 simultaneous contact pairs across multiple objects. It also integrates a sampling strategy: many candidate end-effector positions are sampled, each gets its own C3+ solve, and the lowest-cost candidate is executed. This helps escape local minima that trip up pure local MPC.

OSC: the low-level tracker

OSC — Operational Space Controller

C3/C3+ outputs a desired end-effector trajectory \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}(t)\). OSC converts this into joint torques \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) that the robot's motors actually apply. This is exactly the \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \mathbf{f}\) mapping from §0, extended with a nullspace term to handle redundant joints:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \mathbf{f}_\text{des} + \underbrace{(I - J^\top J^{+\top})}_{\text{nullspace projector}} \boldsymbol{\tau}_0 \]

\(\mathbf{f}_\text{des}\) is the desired Cartesian force at the end-effector (computed from a PD law on the tracking error). \(J^{+}\) is the pseudoinverse of \(J\). The nullspace term \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_0\) handles joint-level objectives (e.g. staying away from joint limits) without disturbing the end-effector task.

In Push Anything, OSC runs on a separate real-time computer dedicated to low-latency robot communication, while C3+ runs on a different machine doing the heavy optimisation. The two communicate over LCM.

The two-level architecture

LevelControllerInputOutputRate
High level C3 / C3+ Current state \(\mathbf{x}\), goal pose Desired end-effector trajectory \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\) ~10–50 Hz
Low level OSC Desired trajectory \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\), joint state \(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}\) Joint torques \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) ~1 kHz

C3/C3+ reasons about what the robot should do with contacts over the next few seconds. OSC handles how the robot's joints should move to make that happen — fast enough to be smooth and stable. The two controllers operate at different timescales and are connected through the desired trajectory.

§8

MuJoCo + Allegro Hand: Will Friction Be Applied, and How Accurate Is It?

Suppose you set up the Allegro hand holding a coloured cube in MuJoCo, and drive the fingers to press inward with a given normal force on each of the 4 lateral faces. Two questions arise: (1) does MuJoCo automatically apply frictional forces, or do you have to specify them? (2) How well does MuJoCo's friction match the contact model assumed by C3/C3+?

Yes — MuJoCo applies friction automatically

You do not have to manually compute or apply friction forces. MuJoCo detects contact between surfaces, computes normal forces, and resolves friction as part of its physics step. What you specify is:

  • The friction coefficient \(\mu\) (set per geom or pair in the MJCF/XML: friction="1.0 0.005 0.0001", where the three values are sliding, torsional, and rolling friction)
  • Contact stiffness and damping (via solref and solimp)

Given those, MuJoCo will automatically determine at each timestep whether each contact is sticking or sliding, and will apply the appropriate friction force — you just apply joint torques or position targets to the fingers.

How MuJoCo models contact — and how it differs from C3

MuJoCo and C3 both model Coulomb friction, but they use fundamentally different mathematical formulations. This matters for how accurately MuJoCo friction matches what C3 assumes.

How accurate is MuJoCo friction for your scenario?

Where MuJoCo is accurate enough

For quasi-static grasping — fingers pressing slowly inward, cube held nearly still — MuJoCo's friction is a good approximation of Coulomb friction. The magnitude of friction forces, the sticking/sliding boundary, and the direction of slip all behave correctly when velocities are small. This regime is where most grasp-stability analysis lives, and MuJoCo is reliable here.

Where discrepancies appear

For dynamic rotation of the cube (the task you want), several sources of error enter:

  • Polyhedral vs. elliptic cone: C3 approximates the friction cone as a 4-sided pyramid. MuJoCo uses an elliptic cone. For forces near the cone boundary (sliding is about to occur), these differ by up to ~30% in direction depending on the sliding angle.
  • Soft vs. rigid contact: MuJoCo's spring-damper normal force depends on penetration depth and velocity, not just contact state. During fast rotation, the effective normal force in MuJoCo will oscillate slightly, changing the friction budget \(\mu f_n\) in ways the rigid LCS model does not predict.
  • Torsional friction: Rotating the cube about the contact normal (spinning) involves torsional friction. MuJoCo models this with a separate coefficient (friction's second parameter). C3's LCS formulation for planar pushing does not explicitly model torsional friction — it uses only the sliding cone.
  • Allegro fingertip geometry: Allegro fingertips have a rounded dome shape, not a point contact. MuJoCo will resolve this as a small contact patch with distributed forces. The LCS model typically assumes a point contact with a single contact normal. This mismatch grows when the cube rotates and the contact patch shifts.
What this means for your simulation plan

If you apply joint torques to press Allegro fingers against the cube's 4 faces and then apply a reorienting wrench, MuJoCo will produce physically plausible friction — the cube will not just slide freely. However, the friction forces MuJoCo computes will not exactly match the forces that C3's LCS model would predict for the same scenario, for three reasons:

  1. Different friction cone geometry (elliptic vs. pyramidal)
  2. Different normal force model (soft penalty vs. rigid complementarity)
  3. Point contact assumption vs. Allegro's extended contact patch

For a first-pass simulation to verify that grasp force decomposition works and that you can rotate the cube, MuJoCo is entirely appropriate. To validate a specific C3-based controller, the sim-to-real gap between MuJoCo's smooth model and the LCS rigid model is a known source of discrepancy — the C3 paper explicitly notes this is why uniqueness of the state mapping must be assumed rather than derived.

Practical recommendation

Set friction="1.0 0.005 0.0001" on the Allegro fingertip geoms and cube geom in your MJCF. Use a stiff contact model (solref="0.002 1") to minimise penetration and get closer to the rigid-body assumption in C3. Apply finger joint torques (not position control) to maintain a steady inward normal force. MuJoCo will then handle friction automatically, and the simulation will be physically meaningful even if not perfectly matched to the LCS contact model.

§9

The Complete Control Flow — Five Clarifications

This section works through five questions about the control architecture in order, building on each answer before the next. Read them in sequence.

Question 1

The objective says "minimise trajectory error in x". But when grasping, x doesn't change — so how can there be nonzero torques?

The confusion is that forces are not part of the state \(\mathbf{x}\). Let's be precise about what each symbol means.

What \(\mathbf{x}\) is

The state vector stacks positions and velocities — nothing else:

\[ \mathbf{x}_k = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{q}_k \\ \dot{\mathbf{q}}_k \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \]

where \(\mathbf{q}_k\) contains robot joint angles and the cube's position + orientation (as a quaternion), and \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}_k\) contains their time derivatives. No forces. No torques.

Where forces live

Forces appear in two separate places:

SymbolNameWhat it is
\(\mathbf{u}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_u}\) Control input What the robot commands at timestep \(k\). Could be joint torques, or desired Cartesian force at the end-effector. This is the robot's decision variable.
\(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\lambda}\) Contact forces The normal and friction forces at each contact, determined by the physics (complementarity constraints). The robot does not directly set these — they arise from how the fingers press.

The dynamics equation shows the answer

The LCS dynamics are:

\[ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{x}_k + B\mathbf{u}_k + D\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + \mathbf{d} \]

Now suppose we are grasping statically: the cube is held, nothing is moving, so \(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k = \mathbf{x}^*\). Substituting:

\[ \mathbf{x}^* = A\mathbf{x}^* + B\mathbf{u}_k + D\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + \mathbf{d} \] \[ \Longrightarrow \quad B\mathbf{u}_k + D\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k = (I - A)\mathbf{x}^* - \mathbf{d} \]

The right-hand side is generally nonzero (gravity acts, contact forces act). This means \(\mathbf{u}_k \neq \mathbf{0}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k \neq \mathbf{0}\) are required to hold the system in equilibrium, even though \(\mathbf{x}\) is not changing. Zero change in state does not mean zero force. It means the forces perfectly balance, which requires them to be present and nonzero.

Intuition

Stand still. Your muscles are applying force (against gravity) even though your position is constant. The same is true for the robot: it must exert torques at the joints to maintain the grasp even when nothing is moving. The objective minimises the deviation from the desired state and the control effort. It does not say "apply zero torque when position is not changing."

Question 2

Does the C3 objective function include any term about maintaining grasp contact — i.e. keeping normal forces above zero?

The standard C3/C3+ objective as published is:

\[ \min \;\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Bigl(\mathbf{x}_k^\top Q\, \mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{u}_k^\top R\, \mathbf{u}_k\Bigr) + \mathbf{x}_N^\top Q_N \mathbf{x}_N \]

\(Q, Q_N \succeq 0\) penalise state error relative to the goal. \(R \succ 0\) penalises large control effort. There is no explicit term on \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\).

Contact forces are not directly optimised — they are determined entirely by the complementarity constraints (the physics). The optimiser chooses \(\mathbf{u}_k\) to minimise state error; the resulting \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) is whatever the contact equations produce.

Important: C3 is for pushing, not grasping

The C3 and Push Anything papers use a single spherical end-effector that pushes objects on a table. Contact is made and broken naturally as the end-effector moves. There is no need to maintain a grasp.

For an Allegro hand grasping a cube, you would need to add to the formulation:

  • A constraint: \(\lambda_n^i \geq f_{\min} > 0\) for each finger contact \(i\), ensuring normal force never drops to zero (contact never breaks).
  • Or a penalty term: \(-\alpha \sum_i \lambda_n^i\) in the objective, rewarding larger normal forces.
  • Or an inner force layer: apply a constant \(\mathbf{f}_\text{internal} \in \ker(G)\) separately (as in §4), and run C3 only for the task forces on top of it.

This is an open design choice. The published C3 formulation does not handle it.

Questions 3 & 4

Who solves the problem at each timestep, and what exactly are the steps? Also: how does the polyhedral cone approximation work?

We first set up the full optimization problem, then walk through how ADMM breaks it down, and along the way explain the pyramid.

Step 0 — Linearize to get the LCS matrices

At the current estimated state \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) and nominal input \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\), linearise the full nonlinear contact dynamics (Equation (5)-(7) in C3) to get:

\[ A,\, B,\, D,\, E,\, F,\, H,\, \mathbf{c},\, \mathbf{d} \quad\in\quad \mathbb{R}^{(\text{appropriate dimensions})} \]

These are computed once and held fixed for the duration of this timestep's solve.

Step 1 — Write the contact force vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) (and the pyramid)

Before writing the full problem, we need to say exactly what \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) contains. There are \(n_c\) contact pairs (e.g., each fingertip-cube pair). For each contact \(i\), the forces are:

  • \(\lambda_n^i \geq 0\) — a single scalar normal force (pushing inward along the contact normal)
  • \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^i \in \mathbb{R}^{n_e}\), \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^i \geq \mathbf{0}\) — \(n_e\) friction force components, one per pyramid edge
  • \(\gamma^i \geq 0\) — a scalar slack variable encoding whether the contact is sliding

The polyhedral (pyramid) approximation of the friction cone

True Coulomb friction says the friction force \(\mathbf{f}_t \in \mathbb{R}^2\) (two tangential directions at the contact) satisfies:

\[ \|\mathbf{f}_t\| \leq \mu\, \lambda_n \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \sqrt{f_{tx}^2 + f_{ty}^2} \leq \mu\, \lambda_n \]

This is a circular cone in \((f_{tx}, f_{ty}, \lambda_n)\) space. At constant \(\lambda_n\), the feasible \(\mathbf{f}_t\) is a disc of radius \(\mu\lambda_n\). The square root makes this nonlinear — we cannot put it into a linear program.

The fix: replace the disc with a regular \(n_e\)-sided polygon. For \(n_e = 4\) this is a square. Define \(n_e\) unit vectors evenly spaced in the tangential plane:

\[ \mathbf{d}_j = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\!\tfrac{2\pi j}{n_e}\\\sin\!\tfrac{2\pi j}{n_e}\end{pmatrix}, \quad j = 0,\ldots, n_e{-}1 \]

For \(n_e = 4\): \(\mathbf{d}_0 = (1,0)\), \(\mathbf{d}_1 = (0,1)\), \(\mathbf{d}_2 = (-1,0)\), \(\mathbf{d}_3 = (0,-1)\) — the four axis directions.

The friction force is now parametrized as a nonneg combination of these directions:

\[ \mathbf{f}_t = \sum_{j=0}^{n_e-1} \beta_j\, \mathbf{d}_j = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{d}_0 & \mathbf{d}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{d}_{n_e-1}\end{bmatrix}}_{E_t^\top \;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{2\times n_e}} \boldsymbol{\beta}, \quad \boldsymbol{\beta} \geq \mathbf{0} \]

The cone constraint \(\|\mathbf{f}_t\| \leq \mu\lambda_n\) is now approximated by the linear constraint:

\[ \mathbf{e}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta} \leq \mu\, \lambda_n \qquad \text{where } \mathbf{e} = (1,\ldots,1)^\top \]

This says the total friction budget allocated across all pyramid edges cannot exceed \(\mu\lambda_n\). It is a linear inequality — no square roots. The pyramid is a conservative approximation of the disc; forces near the diagonal of the square are underestimated by a factor of up to \(\cos(\pi/n_e)\) (for \(n_e = 4\), this is \(\sim 0.71\), a 29% error in the worst case).

Complementarity conditions for the pyramid (Stewart-Trinkle)

The full contact model for one contact pair uses three complementarity conditions:

\(\textbf{(i) Contact / separation:}\quad 0 \leq \lambda_n^i \;\perp\; \phi^i(\mathbf{q}) \geq 0\)

\(\phi^i(\mathbf{q}) \geq 0\) is the gap (distance) between the two bodies. If the bodies are separated (\(\phi > 0\)), the normal force is zero (\(\lambda_n = 0\)). If they are in contact (\(\phi = 0\)), the normal force can be positive. One or the other is always zero. That is what \(\perp\) means.

\(\textbf{(ii) Sliding vs. sticking:}\quad 0 \leq \gamma^i \;\perp\; (\mu\lambda_n^i - \mathbf{e}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}^i) \geq 0\)

\(\gamma^i\) is the sliding speed magnitude. The term \(\mu\lambda_n^i - \mathbf{e}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}^i\) is the remaining friction budget. If there is remaining budget, we are sticking (\(\gamma = 0\)). If the full budget is used (\(\mu\lambda_n = \mathbf{e}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\)), we are sliding (\(\gamma > 0\)). Again, exactly one side is nonzero.

\(\textbf{(iii) Friction direction:}\quad 0 \leq \boldsymbol{\beta}^i \;\perp\; (E_t J_t^i \dot{\mathbf{q}} + \gamma^i \mathbf{e}) \geq 0\)

\(E_t J_t^i \dot{\mathbf{q}}\) is the contact point's tangential velocity projected onto each pyramid edge direction. If the contact point is moving in direction \(\mathbf{d}_j\), then \(\beta_j > 0\) (friction opposes that motion). If the contact is sticking (\(\gamma = 0\)), the velocity term is zero and the complementarity reduces to \(\boldsymbol{\beta} \geq 0\) free.

All three conditions together (for all \(n_c\) contacts) form the complementarity variable vector:

\[ \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k^n \\ \boldsymbol{\beta}_k \\ \boldsymbol{\gamma}_k\end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_c(1 + n_e + 1)} \]

and the gap function stacks all the complementarity right-hand sides into \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_k\).

Step 2 — Write the full QPCC

Substituting the LCS matrices and the contact variable definition, the problem is:

\(\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}_{0:N},\,\mathbf{u}_{0:N-1},\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0:N-1}} \quad \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_k^\top Q\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{u}_k^\top R\mathbf{u}_k\bigr) + \mathbf{x}_N^\top Q_N\mathbf{x}_N\)

\(\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{x}_k + B\mathbf{u}_k + D\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + \mathbf{d} \quad\forall k \quad\text{[linear dynamics]}\)

\(\phantom{\text{s.t.}\quad} \boldsymbol{\eta}_k = E\mathbf{x}_k + F\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + H\mathbf{u}_k + \mathbf{c} \quad\forall k \quad\text{[gap/slack definition]}\)

\(\phantom{\text{s.t.}\quad} 0 \leq \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k \;\perp\; \boldsymbol{\eta}_k \geq 0 \quad\forall k \quad\text{[complementarity — the hard part]}\)

This is a Quadratic Program with Complementarity Constraints (QPCC). It is not a standard QP because of the complementarity condition — that condition is non-convex and combinatorially hard (every contact at every timestep is either on or off). With \(N\) timesteps and \(n_c\) contacts, there are \(2^{N \cdot n_c}\) possible contact mode sequences.

Step 3 — The consensus reformulation (making ADMM applicable)

Introduce a copy of the decision variables. Define:

\[ \mathbf{z}_k = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}_k \\ \mathbf{u}_k \\ \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k \\ \boldsymbol{\eta}_k\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\delta}_k = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\delta}_k^x \\ \boldsymbol{\delta}_k^u \\ \boldsymbol{\delta}_k^\lambda \\ \boldsymbol{\delta}_k^\eta\end{pmatrix} \quad\text{(a copy of }\mathbf{z}_k\text{)} \]

Assign the constraints to two sets:

SetWhat it encodesAssigned to
\(\mathcal{D}\) Linear dynamics, gap definition, initial condition, input bounds — all linear constraints \(\mathbf{z}\) (the original variables)
\(\mathcal{H}_k\) Complementarity: \(0 \leq \boldsymbol{\delta}_k^\lambda \perp \boldsymbol{\delta}_k^\eta \geq 0\) — the nonlinear / combinatorial part \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_k\) (the copy variables), independently per timestep \(k\)

The QPCC is rewritten as:

\(\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z},\,\boldsymbol{\delta}}\; c(\mathbf{z}) + \mathcal{I}_\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}) + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}_k}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_k)\)

\(\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf{z}_k = \boldsymbol{\delta}_k \quad \forall k\)

where \(\mathcal{I}_S(\cdot)\) is the indicator function of set \(S\): it equals 0 if the argument is in \(S\), and \(+\infty\) otherwise. The constraint \(\mathbf{z}_k = \boldsymbol{\delta}_k\) enforces that the two copies agree. At a solution, \(\mathbf{z}\) satisfies the dynamics and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) satisfies complementarity — and they are the same thing.

Step 4 — ADMM: three alternating updates

Form the augmented Lagrangian, which adds a quadratic penalty for the consensus constraint \(\mathbf{z}_k = \boldsymbol{\delta}_k\):

\[ \mathcal{L}_\rho(\mathbf{z}, \boldsymbol{\delta}, \mathbf{w}) = c(\mathbf{z}) + \mathcal{I}_\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}) + \sum_k \mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}_k}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_k) + \frac{\rho}{2}\sum_k \|\mathbf{z}_k - \boldsymbol{\delta}_k + \mathbf{w}_k\|^2 \]

\(\mathbf{w}_k\) are the scaled dual variables (Lagrange multipliers). \(\rho > 0\) is a penalty weight. ADMM cycles through three updates:

Update 1 — z-update (the QP step)
\[\mathbf{z}^{i+1} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{z} \in \mathcal{D}}\;\Bigl[c(\mathbf{z}) + \tfrac{\rho}{2}\sum_k\|\mathbf{z}_k - \boldsymbol{\delta}_k^i + \mathbf{w}_k^i\|^2\Bigr]\]

This is a standard QP: quadratic cost (the original objective plus a quadratic proximity penalty to \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^i\)) with the linear constraints from \(\mathcal{D}\) (dynamics, bounds). The complementarity constraint is gone — it has been moved to the \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) side. A standard QP solver (e.g. OSQP) handles this. Across the full \(N\)-step horizon, this solves for all \(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k, \boldsymbol{\eta}_k\) simultaneously.

Update 2 — δ-update (the projection step)
\[\boldsymbol{\delta}_k^{i+1} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\delta}_k \in \mathcal{H}_k}\;\tfrac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{z}_k^{i+1} - \boldsymbol{\delta}_k + \mathbf{w}_k^i\|^2 \quad \forall k\]

Each timestep \(k\) is independent, so all \(N\) can be solved in parallel. For each \(k\), this is the Euclidean projection of \((\mathbf{z}_k^{i+1} + \mathbf{w}_k^i)\) onto the complementarity set \(\mathcal{H}_k = \{(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) : \mathbf{a} \geq \mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{b} \geq \mathbf{0},\, \mathbf{a} \odot \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}\}\).

How the projection works — per element

For each scalar pair \((\lambda^\circ,\, \eta^\circ)\), the nearest point on the L-shaped set \(\{(a,b): a\geq 0,\, b\geq 0,\, ab = 0\}\) is:

\[ (\hat\lambda,\, \hat\eta) = \begin{cases} (\lambda^\circ,\; 0) & \text{if } \lambda^\circ \geq \eta^\circ \geq 0 \\ (0,\; \eta^\circ) & \text{if } \eta^\circ > \lambda^\circ \geq 0 \\ (\lambda^\circ,\; 0) & \text{if } \lambda^\circ \geq 0,\; \eta^\circ < 0 \\ (0,\; \eta^\circ) & \text{if } \eta^\circ \geq 0,\; \lambda^\circ < 0 \\ (0,\; 0) & \text{if } \lambda^\circ < 0 \text{ and } \eta^\circ < 0 \end{cases} \]

In words: if both are positive, assign the larger one to its axis and set the other to zero. If one is negative, clamp it to zero and keep the other. If both are negative, both go to zero. This is a trivial closed-form computation — no solver needed.

Update 3 — w-update (dual variable update)
\[\mathbf{w}_k^{i+1} = \mathbf{w}_k^i + \mathbf{z}_k^{i+1} - \boldsymbol{\delta}_k^{i+1} \quad \forall k\]

This is just addition. The dual variable \(\mathbf{w}_k\) accumulates the disagreement between \(\mathbf{z}_k\) and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_k\). As the algorithm converges, \(\mathbf{z}_k \to \boldsymbol{\delta}_k\) and \(\mathbf{w}_k\) stabilises.

What comes out and what the robot does with it

After convergence (or a fixed number of ADMM iterations), extract \(\mathbf{u}_0\) (the control input for the current timestep) and apply it. Discard the rest of the horizon — at the next control step, the whole procedure runs again from the new state \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\). This is standard receding-horizon MPC.

\[ \underbrace{\hat{\mathbf{x}} \to \text{LCS} \to \text{ADMM}}_{\text{C3/C3+: high-level planner}} \xrightarrow{\mathbf{u}_0,\;\text{desired traj}} \underbrace{\text{OSC}}_{\text{low-level}} \xrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\tau}} \text{Robot} \xrightarrow{\text{sensors}} \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\text{new} \to \cdots \]
Question 5

C3 gives a trajectory of states x. If x is constant during grasping, how does OSC apply a nonzero fingertip force?

This is the most important practical question for the Allegro setup. The short answer: OSC in the papers is pure position tracking — but for grasping you need to run it in a hybrid position + force mode.

OSC in pure position tracking mode (what the papers do)

For the pushing papers, OSC takes the desired end-effector position \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\) from C3 and outputs joint torques:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \underbrace{\Bigl[K_p(\mathbf{x}^\text{des} - \mathbf{x}) + K_d(\dot{\mathbf{x}}^\text{des} - \dot{\mathbf{x}})\Bigr]}_{\mathbf{f}_\text{pos}: \text{ PD feedback on position error}} + \underbrace{(I - J^\top J^{+\top})\boldsymbol{\tau}_0}_{\text{nullspace term}} \]

When position is matched (\(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^\text{des}\)): \(\mathbf{f}_\text{pos} = \mathbf{0}\), so \(\boldsymbol{\tau} \approx \mathbf{0}\) (only the nullspace term remains). No grasp force is maintained. This works for the pushing case because the end-effector naturally presses against objects by being driven to positions that overlap them (the contact force comes from position error being small but nonzero at the contact surface).

Why this breaks for grasping

If you drive the Allegro fingers to their grasp positions and then hold them there with pure position OSC, the moment there is zero position error, the joint torques drop toward zero — and the fingers stop pressing. Gravity pulls the cube down, friction is lost, and the cube falls. Pure position control cannot maintain a static grasp force.

What you need: hybrid position + force control

Extend OSC with an explicit force setpoint \(\mathbf{f}_\text{grasp}\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \Bigl[\underbrace{K_p(\mathbf{x}^\text{des} - \mathbf{x}) + K_d(\dot{\mathbf{x}}^\text{des} - \dot{\mathbf{x}})}_{\mathbf{f}_\text{pos}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{f}_\text{grasp}}_{\text{constant squeeze force}}\Bigr] + (I - J^\top J^{+\top})\boldsymbol{\tau}_0 \]

\(\mathbf{f}_\text{grasp}\) is the desired contact force at the fingertip — it lives in \(\ker(G)\) (internal force, §4), so it squeezes the cube without accelerating it. It is nonzero even when position is not changing. The torques needed to maintain it:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau}_\text{grasp} = J^\top \mathbf{f}_\text{grasp} \neq \mathbf{0} \quad\text{even when } \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^\text{des} \]

§10

How Contact Force Is Implicit in the Desired Position

This is a subtle but central idea. We build it up from scratch, starting with what OSC actually computes, then showing what changes the moment the end-effector touches an object.

Case 1 — End-effector in free space (no contact)

OSC computes a desired Cartesian force at the end-effector from a PD law on the position error, then maps it to joint torques:

\[ \mathbf{f}_\text{tip} = K_p\,(\mathbf{x}^\text{des} - \mathbf{x}) + K_d\,(\dot{\mathbf{x}}^\text{des} - \dot{\mathbf{x}}) \qquad \boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \mathbf{f}_\text{tip} \]

In free space nothing blocks the end-effector, so it moves toward \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\). As it arrives:

\[ \mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{x}^\text{des} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{f}_\text{tip} \to \mathbf{0} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boldsymbol{\tau} \to \mathbf{0} \]

No contact, no force. Nothing surprising here.

Case 2 — End-effector touches an object

Now suppose the end-effector reaches the surface of the object at position \(\mathbf{x}_\text{surf}\) and C3 has commanded a desired position \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\) that lies past the surface — i.e. slightly inside the object:

\[ \mathbf{x}^\text{des} = \mathbf{x}_\text{surf} + \underbrace{\delta}_{\text{desired penetration depth}} \]

The object is solid. The end-effector cannot pass through it. So the actual end-effector position is locked at the surface:

\[ \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_\text{surf} \quad\text{(held here by the contact)} \]

Now compute the PD force:

\[ \mathbf{f}_\text{tip} = K_p\,(\mathbf{x}^\text{des} - \mathbf{x}) = K_p\,(\mathbf{x}_\text{surf} + \delta - \mathbf{x}_\text{surf}) = K_p\,\delta \]

This force is nonzero and persistent. The end-effector never reaches \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\) because the object blocks it. The position error \(\delta\) never goes to zero. The PD controller keeps pushing — and that push is exactly the contact force transmitted into the object.

The implicit relationship

Contact force = stiffness × desired penetration

\[ \underbrace{f_\text{contact}}_{\text{force on object}} \;=\; K_p \cdot \underbrace{\delta}_{\text{how far past the surface } \mathbf{x}^\text{des} \text{ is set}} \]

The contact force is not explicitly commanded. It is a consequence of where you placed the desired position. Setting \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\) deeper into the object increases \(\delta\), which increases the contact force. Setting \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\) exactly at the surface gives \(\delta = 0\) and zero contact force.

How C3 exploits this for pushing

C3 plans a trajectory of desired end-effector positions \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}_k\). When the trajectory drives the end-effector into contact with an object, \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}_k\) sits slightly past the object surface. The position error that OSC sees is \(\delta_k\), and by the stiffness relationship above, the contact force on the object is:

\[ f_\text{contact} = K_p\,\delta_k \]

C3 does not need to reason about this force explicitly in the objective — the force is a byproduct of the planned position. Larger \(\delta_k\) (deeper desired penetration) means a harder push. To push gently, C3 sets \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\) barely past the surface. To push hard, it sets it further in.

What the contact model in C3 does here

Inside the C3 planner, \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) (the contact force) is determined by the complementarity constraint — it is the force that the object surface exerts on the end-effector to prevent penetration. C3 plans \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\) and \(\mathbf{u}^\text{des}\) such that the resulting \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) is large enough to push the object toward the goal. The OSC then ensures the robot physically produces that position trajectory, and the contact force emerges automatically.

Why this mechanism breaks for dexterous grasping

For pushing there is one end-effector and one contact. For the Allegro hand grasping a cube there are four fingers, each pressing from a different face. The implicit force mechanism breaks in three ways:

1

The cube can move — and all four position errors change simultaneously

For pushing, the object moves in one direction (away from the push). The position error \(\delta\) stays well-defined because the end-effector and the object surface move together.

For grasping, if the cube shifts even slightly — say, it drops 1 mm due to gravity — then all four contact surfaces move at once. Fingers on the top face suddenly have larger \(\delta\) (more force), fingers on the bottom face have smaller \(\delta\) (less force). The four contact forces become coupled through the cube's motion and start fighting each other. There is no stable equilibrium from pure position control with four opposing fingers.

2

The contact force direction is also implicit — and wrong for grasping

For pushing, the contact force direction is always the same as the end-effector's approach direction. This is consistent: you push in one direction.

For grasping, each finger must apply a precisely inward normal force. The implicit direction is determined by whichever way the PD law pulls toward \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\). If the fingertip is slightly misaligned, the implicit force has a tangential component that tries to slide along the cube face — which the cube resists, creating unwanted moments. The direction is not guaranteed to be purely normal.

3

There is no way to set internal force independently of task force

For pushing, there is only one contact and one "task": push. The single \(\delta\) controls the single contact force, and that force is the task force.

For grasping, we need to decompose contact forces into internal (§4: squeeze, lives in \(\ker(G)\), keeps the grasp stable) and task (produces the desired wrench on the cube). With implicit position control, there is no way to set these two components separately. Any change in \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}\) for one finger changes both the internal and the task component of that finger's force simultaneously.

Summary

For pushing: one contact, one direction, force = \(K_p \times \delta\) where \(\delta\) is the single well-defined gap between end-effector and object. Contact force is a stable byproduct of position. OSC works perfectly.

For grasping: four contacts, four directions, all coupled through the cube's six degrees of freedom. Cube motion changes all four \(\delta\)s at once in a coupled, unstable way. Contact forces are no longer a stable byproduct of position — they must be controlled explicitly through a force setpoint, which is what the hybrid position + force OSC in §9 Q5 provides.

§11

How Push Anything Encodes the Goal and Solves It Across Distance

We trace the full control loop in Push Anything from scratch — what the state contains, how the goal enters the cost, what the optimizer does when the robot is far from the object, what changes the moment contact is made, how the complementarity constraint resolves that transition, and how the final object pose is guaranteed.

1 — What the state x contains and where the goal lives

The full state at timestep \(k\) is:

\[ \mathbf{x}_k = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{p}_k^e \\ \mathbf{p}_k^o \\ \boldsymbol{\theta}_k^o \\[4pt] \dot{\mathbf{p}}_k^e \\ \dot{\mathbf{p}}_k^o \\ \dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_k^o \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \]

where \(\mathbf{p}^e \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the end-effector (Cartesian) position, \(\mathbf{p}^o \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the object's centre-of-mass position, \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^o \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the object's orientation (e.g. Euler angles or a rotation vector), and the dotted terms are their velocities. In the 1-object setting, \(n_x = 19\) (3 end-effector + 6 object pose + 3 end-effector velocity + 6 object velocity + 1 slack for contact geometry = 19; the paper quotes this number directly). For \(n\) objects the state grows by 12 per additional object.

The goal is a desired state:

\[ \mathbf{x}^\text{goal} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{p}^{e,\text{nom}} \\ \mathbf{p}^{o,\text{goal}} \\ \boldsymbol{\theta}^{o,\text{goal}} \\ \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} \end{pmatrix} \]

The object components are set to the desired goal pose (specified by the user or a task planner). The velocity components are zero (we want the object to arrive still). The end-effector component \(\mathbf{p}^{e,\text{nom}}\) is a soft nominal position — it matters much less than the object pose in the cost weights, and it is overridden by the sampling layer anyway. The goal is entirely about where the object ends up, not about where the end-effector ends up.

2 — The cost function: how the goal pulls the optimizer

Define the error relative to the goal: \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k = \mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{x}^\text{goal}\). The objective is:

\[ \min \;\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k^\top Q_k\, \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k + \mathbf{u}_k^\top R_k\, \mathbf{u}_k\Bigr) + \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_N^\top Q_N\, \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_N \]

\(Q_k\) and \(Q_N\) are diagonal positive-semidefinite matrices. Their diagonal entries are the weights on each component of the state error. The object pose components get large weights; the end-effector position components get small weights (or zero). So the optimizer cares overwhelmingly about whether the object reaches its goal pose, and only mildly about where the end-effector sits. \(R_k \succ 0\) penalises large control forces (keeps the robot from being unnecessarily aggressive).

\(Q_N\) (the terminal weight) is typically set much larger than the stage weights \(Q_k\). This concentrates the pull toward the goal at the end of the planning horizon, which encourages the optimizer to find trajectories that arrive close to the goal after \(N\) steps.

3 — The three-layer architecture

Before tracing the physics, it helps to see all three layers and what problem each one solves:

LayerNameInputOutputSolves
Layer 1 Sampling Current object pose, goal pose, object mesh Best candidate end-effector position \(\mathbf{p}^{e,*}\) Global guidance: "where should the end-effector be to make progress?"
Layer 2 C3+ (CI-MPC) \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), \(\mathbf{p}^{e,*}\) as initial end-effector setpoint, goal \(\mathbf{x}^\text{goal}\) Desired end-effector trajectory \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}_{0:N}\), contact forces \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0:N-1}\) Local contact-aware planning: "given we're here, what forces to apply?"

Layers 2 and 3 run at every control step. Layer 1 re-evaluates whenever the current candidate is no longer making progress.

4 — When the robot and object are far apart: the sampling layer rescues the optimizer

Suppose the object is 0.5 m away from the end-effector. What does C3+ see? The gap function \(\Phi\) between the end-effector and the object is large and positive. The complementarity constraint says:

\[ 0 \leq \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k \;\perp\; \Phi(\mathbf{x}_k) \geq 0 \]

Since \(\Phi \gg 0\) (large gap), the complementarity forces \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k = 0\) for all \(k\) in the horizon. No contact forces exist. The problem degenerates to a pure state-tracking QP with no coupling to the object at all:

\[ \min \;\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k^\top Q_k \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k + \mathbf{u}_k^\top R_k \mathbf{u}_k\Bigr) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{x}_k + B\mathbf{u}_k + \mathbf{d},\quad \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k = \mathbf{0} \]

The optimizer can only move the end-effector. But the object's state cost is in \(Q_k\) — and \(\mathbf{u}_k\) has no effect on the object when \(\boldsymbol{\lambda} = \mathbf{0}\). So the object error in the cost is fixed no matter what \(\mathbf{u}_k\) is chosen. The gradient of the cost with respect to \(\mathbf{u}_k\) says nothing about the object. The optimizer is stuck in a local minimum: it cannot see a path that reduces object error because the horizon is too short to bridge the distance.

Why pure local MPC fails for distant objects

The LCS model is a local linearisation valid only near the current state. When the object is far away, the linearisation window does not reach the object. Within the planning horizon \(N \cdot \Delta t\) seconds, the end-effector physically cannot reach the object. The cost gradient with respect to all controls is dominated by the end-effector regularisation term \(R_k\), which just says "do nothing." The controller freezes or drifts randomly.

How sampling solves the far-away problem

Instead of running C3+ from the current end-effector position, Push Anything samples \(S\) candidate end-effector positions around the object surface:

  1. Pick a random face of the object mesh (weighted by area)
  2. Project outward along that face's normal by a fixed distance
  3. Project down to a fixed world height (table-top plane)
  4. Reject if too close to any object

All candidates are on or near the object, so starting from any one of them, C3+ is within reach of the object in the planning horizon. For each candidate \(s\), C3+ is solved starting with the end-effector hypothetically at that candidate position. The total cost is:

\[ \text{cost}_s = \underbrace{J_\text{travel}(s)}_{\text{cost to move end-effector to candidate } s} + \underbrace{J_\text{C3+}(s)}_{\text{C3+ objective cost from candidate } s} \]

The candidate with the lowest total cost wins. If it differs from the current end-effector position, the robot first moves there along a collision-free straight-line path (no contact reasoning needed — the object is not in the way), and then C3+ takes over. This is why the paper separates execution into a contact-free repositioning phase and a contact-rich pushing phase.

5 — When the end-effector touches the object: contact forces enter the optimizer

The end-effector has moved to the chosen candidate position. It is now adjacent to the object surface. The gap \(\Phi\) is near zero. At the next control step, C3+ starts a solve where the horizon includes timesteps at which contact is active. Specifically, the complementarity variable \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) can now be nonzero.

The state at contact includes both the end-effector and the object. The dynamics equation that C3+ works with is (from the linearised LCS):

\[ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{p}^e_{k+1} \\ \mathbf{p}^o_{k+1} \\ \boldsymbol{\theta}^o_{k+1} \\ \dot{(\cdots)}_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}_{k+1}} = A\mathbf{x}_k + B\mathbf{u}_k + D\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k + \mathbf{d} \]

The matrix \(D\) is the key. It maps contact forces \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) to changes in the object's position and velocity. Concretely: \(D\) has large entries coupling \(\lambda_n\) (normal contact force) to \(\dot{\mathbf{p}}^o\) (object velocity). So when \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k > 0\), the object accelerates. The optimizer now has a handle on the object.

The cost gradient with respect to \(\mathbf{u}_k\) now has a path to reducing object error:

\[ \frac{\partial \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}}{\partial \mathbf{u}_k} = B + D\,\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\lambda}_k}{\partial \mathbf{u}_k} \;\neq\; \mathbf{0} \]

The term \(D\,\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\partial \mathbf{u}}\) is nonzero as long as contact is active (i.e., as long as \(\Phi \approx 0\) and the complementarity allows force). The optimizer finds the combination of \(\mathbf{u}_k\) (end-effector Cartesian force) and \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\) (contact force at the object surface) that drives the object toward the goal.

6 — How the complementarity constraint resolves the contact transition

At each ADMM step, the δ-update projects \((\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k, \boldsymbol{\eta}_k)\) onto the L-shaped complementarity set, per element. Consider a single contact pair with gap \(\eta = \Phi\) and contact force \(\lambda\):

Situation\(\lambda^\circ\) (force candidate)\(\eta^\circ = \Phi\) (gap candidate)Projection resultPhysical meaning
Far from object \(\approx 0\) (no force needed) \(\gg 0\) (large gap) \(\hat\lambda = 0,\; \hat\eta = \eta^\circ\) Gap wins: contact is off, force is zero
Approaching Small positive Small positive Whichever is larger keeps its value; the other goes to zero Optimizer decides whether to initiate contact this step
In contact \(\gg 0\) (large force needed to move object) \(\approx 0\) (gap is closed) \(\hat\lambda = \lambda^\circ,\; \hat\eta = 0\) Force wins: contact is on, gap is zero
Losing contact Goes to zero (object pulling away) Becomes positive again \(\hat\lambda = 0,\; \hat\eta = \eta^\circ\) Contact drops automatically as gap reopens

No human decision is needed to switch between "contact on" and "contact off." The projection step does it automatically, per element, in closed form, at every ADMM iteration. The optimizer discovers when contact should be made and broken by finding the sequence \((\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k, \boldsymbol{\eta}_k)\) that minimises cost subject to these projections converging.

What "contact-implicit" means

The contact schedule — which contacts are active at which timesteps — is never specified by the user. It is a result of the optimisation. The complementarity constraint encodes the physics that determines when contact is possible, and the ADMM projection enforces it. The optimizer chooses to make contact whenever doing so reduces the cost (i.e., whenever pressing the object actually moves it toward the goal).

7 — What comes out of one C3+ solve and what the robot does with it

After ADMM converges (or is early-terminated after a fixed number of iterations), the solve produces:

  • \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}_{0:N}\) — the planned state trajectory over the horizon
  • \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\text{des}_{0:N-1}\) — the planned contact forces at each step
  • \(\mathbf{u}^\text{des}_{0:N-1}\) — the planned control inputs (Cartesian forces)

Only \(\mathbf{u}_0\) and \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}_0\) are applied immediately (receding horizon). The rest are discarded and recomputed next step.

The low-level impedance controller (OSC) then tracks \(\mathbf{x}^\text{des}_0\) using the full Franka Panda model:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = J_f^\top \Lambda \begin{pmatrix}K & 0 \\ 0 & B\end{pmatrix} \tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e + J_f^\top \tau_{ff} + N_f \bigl(K_n(\mathbf{q}^d_f - \mathbf{q}^f) + B_n(\dot{\mathbf{q}}^d_f - \dot{\mathbf{q}}^f)\bigr) \]

where \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e = \mathbf{x}^{e,\text{des}} - \mathbf{x}^e\) is the end-effector tracking error, \(\Lambda = (J_f M_f^{-1} J_f^\top)^{-1}\) is the end-effector inertia matrix, and the feedforward term \(\tau_{ff} = J_c^\top \boldsymbol{\lambda}^\text{des}\) directly injects the desired contact forces from C3+ as a torque feedforward — this is what prevents the problem in §9 Q5 even for this system: even when position error is zero, \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\text{des}\) keeps the end-effector pressing.

8 — How the final object pose is ensured and maintained

There is no single solve that guarantees the object reaches the goal. Instead, the system relies on three overlapping mechanisms:

Mechanism 1 — Receding horizon feedback

At every control step, the vision system measures the current object pose \((\mathbf{p}^o, \boldsymbol{\theta}^o)\) and feeds it into the new \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\). C3+ re-solves with the updated error \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{x}^\text{goal}\). Any drift, slip, or disturbance is corrected automatically on the next solve — the goal error is always recomputed fresh. The controller never "forgets" where the goal is or accumulates uncorrected error.

Mechanism 2 — Terminal cost Q_N drives convergence

The large terminal weight \(Q_N\) forces the \(N\)-step plan to end near the goal even if intermediate steps are not. Over successive control steps, the receding horizon marches the actual state toward this terminal target. As the object gets closer to the goal, the terminal cost error shrinks, the optimizer needs less aggressive contacts, and the system naturally slows and fine-tunes.

Mechanism 3 — Termination criterion and re-sampling

The object pose is continuously compared against the goal within specified tolerances (position error \(< \epsilon_p\), orientation error \(< \epsilon_\theta\)). Once inside tolerance, the current task is declared complete. If the object overshoots or drifts after reaching the goal, the controller detects the error immediately (the receding horizon cost is no longer near-zero) and begins corrective pushes. The object is not released and forgotten — the controller actively holds it near the goal by continuing to run.

The goal pose is never guaranteed in a single trajectory solve. It is guaranteed by the combination of continuous vision feedback, a cost function that always penalises the current object pose error, and a receding horizon that re-optimises every control step. The system asymptotically converges to the goal as long as the physics are reachable and the solver finds a cost-reducing contact sequence at each step.
